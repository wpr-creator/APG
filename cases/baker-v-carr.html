Baker v. Carr (1962)

Federal courts can hear claims that unequal state legislative districts violate the Fourteenth Amendment.

For your worksheet, find the problem, the voters’ claim, what the Court decided, and what it did not decide. Write a short answer for each—not the whole paragraph.

What happened? Why did voters sue? What did the Court decide? What question did the Court leave open?

What happened?

Tennessee used the same state legislative districts for decades while cities grew. A district with many residents could elect the same number of lawmakers as a district with far fewer residents. City voters therefore had less influence per person.

Why did voters sue?

Charles Baker, a Memphis voter, sued Tennessee’s secretary of state, Joe Carr. Baker argued that the outdated map gave voters in fast-growing districts less influence than voters in less populated districts. He argued that this violated the Equal Protection Clause, the part of the Fourteenth Amendment that requires states to treat people equally under the law.

What did the Court decide?

The Supreme Court ruled 6–2 that a federal court could hear Baker’s claim. Drawing districts involves politics, but judges can still hear a claim that a map violates a constitutional right. A claim a court can hear is called justiciable. For voters in a state that still uses decades-old lines, Baker made it possible to bring their Equal Protection challenge in federal court.

What question did the Court leave open?

The Court left the question of whether Tennessee’s map violated the Constitution for further proceedings. Two years later, Reynolds v. Sims (1964) established the “one person, one vote” rule for state legislatures: state legislative districts must have roughly equal populations.

Read more about the case

Justice Felix Frankfurter dissented, meaning he disagreed with the ruling. He argued that elected officials should handle district boundaries and that judges lacked a clear standard for resolving the dispute.

For claims about maps drawn to favor a political party, Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) held that federal courts cannot decide partisan gerrymandering claims under the federal Constitution. Challenges under state constitutions may still go to state courts.
