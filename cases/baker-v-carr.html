Baker v. Carr (1962)

Federal courts can hear claims that unequal state legislative districts violate the Fourteenth Amendment.

For your worksheet, find the problem, the voters’ claim, what the Court decided, and what it did not decide. Write a short answer for each—not the whole paragraph.

What happened? Why did voters sue? What did the Court decide? What question did the Court leave open?

What happened?

Tennessee kept old district lines while its population shifted, leaving more voters sharing each lawmaker in growing areas.

These districts elected lawmakers to Tennessee’s own House and Senate. Baker challenged the unequal representation.

Why did voters sue?

Baker said his vote had less influence because more people shared his lawmakers. He argued that this violated equal protection.

The Equal Protection Clause is the part of the Fourteenth Amendment requiring states to treat people equally under the law. Charles Baker, a Memphis voter, sued Tennessee’s secretary of state, Joe Carr.

What did the Court decide?

The Court allowed the case to go forward. It did not decide that Tennessee’s map was unconstitutional.

The Court said a federal judge could hear Baker’s claim. A claim a court can hear is justiciable.

The 6–2 ruling sent the case back to a lower court to consider the voters’ claim.

What question did the Court leave open?

The Court left open whether Tennessee’s map violated the Constitution. Baker did not itself require equal populations or order a new map.

Later case: Reynolds v. Sims (1964) established the “one person, one vote” population rule for state legislatures: districts must have roughly equal populations. That later rule should not be credited to Baker.
