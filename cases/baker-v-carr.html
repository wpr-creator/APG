Unit 2

Baker v. Carr (1962)

Federal courts can hear claims that unequal state legislative districts violate the Fourteenth Amendment.

What happened? Why did voters sue? What did the Court decide? What question did the Court leave open? Practice

What happened?

Tennessee used the same state legislative districts for decades while cities grew. A district with many residents could elect the same number of lawmakers as a district with far fewer residents. City voters therefore had less influence per person.

Why did voters sue?

Charles Baker, a Memphis voter, sued Tennessee’s secretary of state, Joe Carr. Baker argued that the outdated map violated the Equal Protection Clause, the part of the Fourteenth Amendment that requires states to treat people equally under the law.

What did the Court decide?

The Supreme Court ruled 6–2 that a federal court could hear Baker’s claim. Drawing districts involves politics, but judges can still hear a claim that a map violates a constitutional right. A claim a court can hear is called justiciable.

What question did the Court leave open?

The Court left the question of whether Tennessee’s map violated the Constitution for further proceedings. The rule requiring roughly equal populations in state legislative districts came two years later in Reynolds v. Sims (1964).

Practice applying the case

Two districts each elect one state representative. One now has four times as many residents as the other. The legislature has kept the same boundaries for decades. A federal judge says courts should stay out of disputes over maps.

Read this second case. In Wesberry v. Sanders (1964), Georgia voters challenged a U.S. House map because their district contained far more people than others. The Supreme Court agreed that the courts could hear the case. It held that Article I, Section 2 requires U.S. House districts to be as equal in population as practicable.

  1. Which case helps you respond to the judge, and which fact matters most?
  2. How did the type of district and the result differ between the two cases?
  3. How did stare decisis, the practice of following precedent, shape the later decision?
Show a sample response

Baker v. Carr applies because the voters allege unequal influence in state legislative districts. Baker allowed federal courts to hear the Equal Protection claim. Wesberry applied Baker’s precedent on court access to a U.S. House map, then used Article I, Section 2 to decide the population issue.

Read more about the case

Justice Felix Frankfurter dissented, meaning he disagreed with the ruling. He argued that elected officials should handle district boundaries and that judges lacked a clear standard for resolving the dispute.

For claims about maps drawn to favor a political party, Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) held that federal courts cannot decide partisan gerrymandering claims under the federal Constitution. Challenges under state constitutions may still go to state courts.
