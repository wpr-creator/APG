Baker v. Carr

369 U.S. 186 · March 26, 1962 · Justice William Brennan
The situation→ The constitutional claim→ What the Court decided→ What it did not decide

📋The situation

Tennessee had not redrawn its legislative districts since 1901. Meanwhile, many people moved from rural areas to cities, but representation did not keep up. Some rural voters had much more representation per person than voters in growing urban areas.

Memphis resident Charles Baker sued Tennessee Secretary of State Joe Carr. He argued that the unequal districts weakened city residents’ voting influence.

❓The constitutional claim

Equal Protection is the Fourteenth Amendment’s promise that government treat people equally under the law.

Baker argued that Tennessee’s outdated districts gave urban residents less voting influence than rural residents, violating the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Question for the Court

Could a federal court hear Baker’s Equal Protection challenge?

⚖️What the Court decided

✓ Federal courts could hear the case — 6 to 2

Justiciable means “a claim a court can hear.” The Court said Baker’s claim met that standard.

Majority: Brennan · Warren · Black · Douglas · Clark · Stewart  |  Dissent: Frankfurter · Harlan
"The right asserted is within the reach of judicial protection under the Fourteenth Amendment."
The Court said federal courts could hear Baker’s claim. It did not decide whether Tennessee’s districts violated the Constitution. The ruling opened the courthouse door to this kind of Equal Protection challenge.

📐What it did not decide

Baker did not rule that Tennessee’s map was unconstitutional or require districts to have equal populations. The Court made the claim hearable; it did not decide whether the claim was right.

The later rule: Reynolds v. Sims (1964)
Two years later, Reynolds v. Sims established the "one person, one vote" rule and required state legislative districts to be apportioned by population. That population-equality rule came from Reynolds, not Baker.

❓Why courts had stayed out

Before Baker, courts had often refused to hear apportionment cases, treating districting as a political question for elected officials. In Colegrove v. Green (1946), Justice Frankfurter warned courts to stay out of the "political thicket" of redistricting.

In Baker, Justice Brennan explained that courts can hear a political-question dispute when the Constitution does not assign it exclusively to another branch and judges have manageable standards for deciding it. He concluded Baker’s claim could be heard.

⚡The dissent — Justice Frankfurter

Justice Frankfurter (joined by Justice Harlan)
Frankfurter's dissent was one of his most passionate — he had spent his career warning courts away from political questions. He argued the majority was dragging courts into the inherently political business of drawing district lines, a task for legislatures and voters, not judges. Courts have no manageable standards for apportionment and no democratic legitimacy to impose their own preferences. "There is not under our Constitution a judicial remedy for every political mischief." His warning proved partially prophetic — redistricting litigation has become one of the most contested and politically charged areas of constitutional law.

🔗Legacy and connections

  • Reynolds v. Sims
    1964    Built directly on Baker — held that both chambers of state legislatures must be apportioned by population. "Legislators represent people, not trees or acres." One person, one vote made explicit.
  • Shaw v. Reno
    1993    Applied Equal Protection to racial gerrymandering. When race predominates in drawing district lines, strict scrutiny applies; the map is not automatically unconstitutional and must be evaluated under that test.
  • Rucho v. Common Cause
    2019    The Court held that partisan-gerrymandering claims under the federal Constitution cannot be decided by federal courts. Rucho did not close off state-court claims under state constitutions or other political avenues. Baker’s ruling still applies to malapportionment claims.
  • Modern impactEvery redistricting cycle following each census triggers Baker-based litigation. After the 2020 census, dozens of states faced legal challenges. Baker created an entire field of election law that fills federal dockets every decade.
Exam focus
Baker is often tested alongside Shaw v. Reno. Baker is about whether federal courts may hear a malapportionment claim; Reynolds established the later one-person, one-vote rule; Shaw addresses racial gerrymandering and strict scrutiny.

Common mistakes

✗Saying Baker established "one person, one vote" — that was Reynolds v. Sims (1964).
✗Thinking Baker ruled Tennessee's districts unconstitutional — it only said federal courts could hear the challenge.
✗Confusing Baker’s ruling about whether a court can hear a claim with Shaw’s racial-gerrymandering claim.
