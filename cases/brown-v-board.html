Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka (1954)

The Supreme Court held that racial segregation in public schools violates the Fourteenth Amendment.

What happened?What was the constitutional issue?What did the Court decide?What are the decision’s limits?

What happened?

Linda Brown and other Black children were denied admission to public schools reserved for white children.

In Topeka, Kansas, the Brown family challenged a school system that assigned children to separate schools by race. Their case reached the Supreme Court with school-segregation cases from South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware. State governments defended separation under the earlier idea of “separate but equal”: separate facilities supposedly satisfied the Constitution if their resources were equal.

What was the constitutional issue?

The families argued that government-enforced racial separation denied children equal protection of the laws.

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to provide equal protection under the law. The question was whether separating public-school students by race violated that protection, even when buildings, teachers, and other physical resources appeared equal.

What did the Court decide?

The Court held that racial segregation in public education is unconstitutional.

Chief Justice Earl Warren explained that separating children by race denied equal educational opportunity. The injury was not just unequal buildings or supplies: state-enforced separation itself made the education unequal. The Court rejected “separate but equal” in public education.

Earl Warren · unanimous decision, 9–0

What are the decision’s limits?

The 1954 decision established the constitutional violation; it did not immediately integrate every school.

The Court addressed how to carry out desegregation in a later decision in 1955. Resistance and enforcement disputes continued for years. For this case, connect a state’s racial separation of public-school students to the Fourteenth Amendment—not simply to whether two schools had similar resources.
