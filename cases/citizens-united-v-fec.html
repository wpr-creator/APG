Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission (2010)

The government cannot ban corporations and unions from spending independently to support or oppose candidates.

What happened?What was the constitutional issue?What did the Court decide?What are the decision’s limits?

What happened?

Citizens United wanted to distribute and advertise a film criticizing Hillary Clinton during the 2008 presidential primaries.

The group was a nonprofit corporation. Federal campaign-finance law restricted corporate funding of certain election-related broadcasts close to an election. The Federal Election Commission, or FEC, enforced that law. Citizens United challenged the restriction on its political message.

What was the constitutional issue?

Could government restrict this independent political spending because the speaker was a corporation?

The First Amendment protects political speech. Independent spending pays for a message without coordinating it with a candidate’s campaign. It differs from a direct contribution, such as giving money to the campaign itself. The government defended the restriction as a way to prevent corruption.

What did the Court decide?

The Court held that government could not ban independent political expenditures on the basis of the speaker’s corporate identity.

The majority reasoned that political speech does not lose First Amendment protection because it comes from a corporation. It also concluded that independent expenditures did not create the kind of direct exchange of money for official favors that could justify the ban. The ruling also affected independent spending by unions.

Anthony Kennedy · spending restriction struck down, 5–4

What are the decision’s limits?

The decision did not remove all campaign-finance rules or authorize unlimited direct donations to candidates.

The Court upheld the challenged disclosure and disclaimer requirements: identifying who paid for a message is different from banning it. Independent spending and campaign contributions remain distinct. The ruling helped expand outside election spending, but it did not by itself decide every rule governing political committees or foreign spending.
