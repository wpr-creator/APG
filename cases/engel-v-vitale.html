Engel v. Vitale (1962)

Public schools cannot organize a government-written prayer for students, even when participation is voluntary.

What happened?What was the constitutional issue?What did the Court decide?What are the decision’s limits?

What happened?

New York officials wrote a prayer for public-school students to recite at the start of the day.

The prayer referred to “Almighty God” but did not name a particular religion. Students could remain silent or be excused. Steven Engel and other parents objected that a government-run school should not lead a government-written religious exercise.

What was the constitutional issue?

The parents argued that a state-sponsored prayer violated the First Amendment’s Establishment Clause.

The Establishment Clause limits government sponsorship of religion. The question was whether the state could compose and promote a prayer in public schools even when participation was voluntary and the prayer was not tied to one denomination.

What did the Court decide?

The Court held that government officials could not write and sponsor this public-school prayer.

The majority explained that composing official prayers is not government’s job. Calling the prayer voluntary or non-denominational did not remove the state’s role in organizing a religious exercise. The First Amendment’s restriction applied to the state through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Hugo Black · decision, 6–1

What are the decision’s limits?

Engel limits government-sponsored prayer; it does not ban students’ private religious expression.

Students retain rights to pray and express religious beliefs, subject to the rules that also apply to other student expression. Distinguish a student choosing to pray from school officials composing or leading an official prayer. This case concerns government establishment of religion, rather than a general prohibition on religion at school.
