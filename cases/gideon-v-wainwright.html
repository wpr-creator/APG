Gideon v. Wainwright (1963)

States must provide a lawyer to defendants who cannot afford one when they are charged with a felony.

What happened?What was the constitutional issue?What did the Court decide?What are the decision’s limits?

What happened?

Clarence Earl Gideon faced a Florida felony charge but could not afford a lawyer.

He asked the trial judge to appoint one. Florida then generally provided appointed counsel only in capital cases—cases in which a defendant could face the death penalty. Gideon represented himself, was convicted, and challenged the denial of a lawyer from prison.

What was the constitutional issue?

Gideon argued that a fair criminal trial required a lawyer even when the defendant could not pay.

The Sixth Amendment protects the right to counsel, meaning a lawyer’s assistance in a criminal case. The question was whether states, as well as the federal government, had to provide that assistance to defendants who could not afford it in felony prosecutions.

What did the Court decide?

The Court held that states must provide a lawyer to defendants who cannot afford one in felony criminal trials.

A lawyer is essential to a fair trial because criminal proceedings involve rules and legal arguments that most defendants cannot handle on their own. The Court applied this Sixth Amendment protection to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause, which protects people against unfair government deprivation of life, liberty, or property.

Hugo Black · unanimous decision, 9–0

What are the decision’s limits?

Gideon concerns appointed counsel in felony criminal prosecutions; it does not promise a free lawyer in every legal dispute.

Applying a Bill of Rights protection to state governments is called selective incorporation. Gideon illustrates that process. After his conviction was overturned, Gideon received a new trial with a lawyer and was acquitted. Rules for other criminal proceedings developed through additional decisions.
