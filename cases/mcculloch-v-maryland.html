McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

Congress could create a national bank to carry out its constitutional powers, and Maryland could not tax that bank.

What happened?What was the constitutional issue?What did the Court decide?What are the decision’s limits?

What happened?

Maryland imposed a tax on a branch of the national bank, and bank cashier James McCulloch refused to pay.

Congress had created the Second Bank of the United States to help carry out federal financial responsibilities. Maryland argued that Congress had no authority to create a bank because the Constitution did not explicitly list a power to establish one.

What was the constitutional issue?

Could Congress create the bank, and could a state tax it in this way?

Congress has enumerated powers—powers listed in the Constitution, such as taxing, borrowing, and regulating commerce. The Necessary and Proper Clause allows Congress to choose appropriate ways to carry out those powers. The Supremacy Clause makes the Constitution and valid federal laws superior to conflicting state laws.

What did the Court decide?

The Court upheld Congress’s power to create the bank and struck down Maryland’s tax on it.

Creating a bank was an appropriate means of carrying out Congress’s financial powers even though the bank itself was not named in the Constitution. This is an implied power: authority that follows from a listed power. Maryland could not use its taxing power to obstruct a valid federal institution.

John Marshall · unanimous decision, 7–0

What are the decision’s limits?

The decision supports implied powers and federal supremacy; it does not give Congress every power or exempt everything connected to government from all taxes.

Congress’s chosen means must serve a legitimate constitutional goal and remain consistent with the Constitution. Federal supremacy protects actions within federal authority. The case is about Maryland’s tax on the national bank, not a rule that any person or business dealing with the federal government never pays state taxes.
