New York Times Co. v. United States (1971)

The federal government did not meet the high legal burden required to stop newspapers from publishing the Pentagon Papers.

What happened?What was the constitutional issue?What did the Court decide?What are the decision’s limits?

What happened?

The New York Times and the Washington Post sought to publish a classified history of U.S. involvement in Vietnam known as the Pentagon Papers.

The Nixon administration asked federal courts to stop further publication, arguing that releasing the material would harm national security. The newspapers argued that the First Amendment protected publication and that government had not justified stopping it.

What was the constitutional issue?

Had the government shown enough reason to block these newspapers before publication?

Stopping expression before it appears is called prior restraint. The First Amendment’s protection of freedom of the press places a heavy burden on government when it seeks such an order. The case asked whether the national-security arguments offered here met that burden.

What did the Court decide?

The Court held that the government had not met its heavy burden of justifying an order stopping publication.

The newspapers could continue publishing the Pentagon Papers. The justices who agreed with the result wrote separate opinions with different reasoning. Do not treat one justice’s strongest language about press freedom or national security as a detailed test adopted by all six.

Per curiam opinion—issued in the Court’s name · decision, 6–3

What are the decision’s limits?

The decision did not declare every prior restraint impossible or give the press blanket immunity for publishing classified information.

The narrow holding is that government failed to justify the restraints sought in this case. Distinguish stopping publication in advance from questions about legal responsibility after publication. A claim of national security did not, by itself, establish a sufficient basis to silence these newspapers.
