Schenck v. United States (1919)

The Court upheld a wartime conviction for distributing leaflets opposing the draft under a speech standard later replaced.

What happened?What was the constitutional issue?What did the Court decide?What are the decision’s limits?

What happened?

During World War I, Charles Schenck distributed leaflets urging men to oppose the military draft.

He was convicted under the Espionage Act for attempting to obstruct military recruitment. Schenck argued that his leaflets were political expression protected by the First Amendment. The case arose during wartime; that setting shaped the Court’s reasoning.

What was the constitutional issue?

Did the First Amendment prevent punishment of these anti-draft leaflets under the law used against Schenck?

The Court considered the words, their setting, and the harm Congress was trying to prevent. It asked whether the expression created a clear and present danger of bringing about that harm. This was the Court’s historical approach in 1919.

What did the Court decide?

The Court upheld Schenck’s conviction, reasoning that these leaflets created a danger of obstructing wartime recruitment.

Holmes explained that the circumstances in which words are used matter. The Court treated the leaflets as an attempt to interfere with recruitment, rather than as protected discussion alone. This describes the Court’s reasoning at that time—not a rule that criticism of war or the draft is automatically a crime.

Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. · unanimous decision, 9–0

What are the decision’s limits?

Schenck’s clear-and-present-danger approach is not the current test for advocacy of unlawful conduct.

A later decision, Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), protects advocacy unless it is directed to producing imminent lawless action and likely to produce it. Imminent means about to happen. Keep the historical ruling separate from the more protective later rule; the familiar theater analogy is not today’s legal test.
