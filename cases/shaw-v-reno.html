Shaw v. Reno

509 U.S. 630 · June 28, 1993 · Justice Sandra Day O'Connor
The situation→ The constitutional claim→ What the Court decided→ What it did not decide

📋The situation

After the 1990 census, North Carolina gained an additional House seat, bringing its total to 12.

North Carolina drew District 12 as a majority-minority district—one where racial or ethnic minority groups make up more than half the residents. It stretched about 160 miles, in places no wider than Interstate 85, connecting Black neighborhoods across several counties. Critics said it looked like a "bug splattered on a windshield."

Ruth Shaw and four other North Carolina voters challenged District 12. They argued the lines had been drawn mainly around race and violated the Fourteenth Amendment.

❓The constitutional claim

Equal Protection is the Fourteenth Amendment’s promise that government treat people equally under the law.

The voters argued that drawing district lines mainly to separate voters by race can violate Equal Protection, even when the state says it wants to improve minority representation.

Question for the Court

Could the voters challenge a district they alleged was drawn mainly around race under the Equal Protection Clause?

⚖️What the Court decided

The Court allowed the claim to proceed

The Court ruled 5–4 that the voters had stated an Equal Protection claim. The case returned to the lower court for further review. The Court did not decide that District 12 was unconstitutional.

Majority: O'Connor · Rehnquist · Scalia · Kennedy · Thomas  |  Dissent: White · Blackmun · Stevens · Souter
"A reapportionment plan that includes in one district individuals who belong to the same race, but who are otherwise widely separated by geographical and political boundaries... bears an uncomfortable resemblance to political apartheid."

A court must apply strict scrutiny if race predominates

Strict scrutiny is the Court’s toughest review: the state must show a compelling reason and a map closely fitted to that purpose.

When race predominates in drawing district lines, strict scrutiny applies. The district’s unusual shape may be evidence that race predominated, but shape alone does not decide whether a map is unconstitutional.

📐What it did not decide

In 1993, the Court did not finally rule District 12 unconstitutional. It did not ban all majority-minority districts, either. It allowed the voters’ claim to move forward; the lower court still had to evaluate the map under strict scrutiny.

Strict scrutiny is a test, not an automatic result
A map can survive strict scrutiny if the state proves a compelling government interest and shows the map is narrowly tailored — closely fitted — to serve it. For example, a state may have a compelling reason to follow federal voting-rights requirements. Race predominance triggers this review; it does not by itself settle the outcome.

❓Why the state added District 12

To comply with Section 5 of the Voting Rights Act, North Carolina submitted an initial map with one majority-Black district. The Justice Department objected, saying the state could create a second district to give Black voters in another region a better opportunity to elect candidates of their choice.

North Carolina revised its map and added a second majority-Black district in the north-central part of the state: District 12. The Justice Department’s objection and the Voting Rights Act help explain why the state drew it.

⚡The dissent — Justice White

Justice White (joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Souter)
The dissenters argued the majority was ignoring the actual harm that apportionment cases involve. Traditional Equal Protection cases involve a majority group diluting minority voting strength — here, white voters were not denied the right to vote, not prevented from running for office, and not disadvantaged in any concrete way. The majority-Black district simply gave Black voters a better chance to elect their preferred candidate, which is exactly what the Voting Rights Act requires. The "political apartheid" rhetoric was inflammatory and unsupported — creating a majority-minority district is categorically different from enforcing racial segregation.

🔗Legacy and connections

  • Baker v. Carr
    1962    Baker allowed federal courts to hear some apportionment challenges. Shaw addressed a different claim: when race predominates in drawing a district, strict scrutiny applies under Equal Protection.
  • Miller v. Johnson
    1995    Clarified Shaw — race must be the "predominant" factor, not just a factor. States can consider race without triggering strict scrutiny as long as traditional redistricting principles (compactness, contiguity, community of interest) are the primary drivers.
  • Rucho v. Common Cause
    2019    The Court held that federal courts cannot decide partisan-gerrymandering claims under the federal Constitution. Rucho did not close off state-court claims under state constitutions or other political avenues.
  • Voting Rights Act
    Tension    The Voting Rights Act requires majority-minority districts in some circumstances. States must follow that law while also meeting Equal Protection requirements; how the rules apply depends on the map and the evidence.
Exam focus
Shaw is often paired with Baker v. Carr in redistricting questions. Baker opened federal courts to certain apportionment claims. Shaw held that when race predominates in drawing district lines, strict scrutiny applies; that alone does not decide whether the map is constitutional.

Common mistakes

✗Saying Shaw automatically invalidated District 12 in 1993 — the Court allowed a claim and sent it back for further review.
✗Thinking race can never be considered — the key question is whether it predominated and, if so, whether the map survives strict scrutiny.
✗Thinking Rucho closed every legal avenue — it held that federal courts cannot decide partisan-gerrymandering claims under the federal Constitution.
