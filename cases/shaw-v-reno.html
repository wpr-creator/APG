Unit 2

Shaw v. Reno (1993)

Voters can challenge a map under the Fourteenth Amendment when they allege that race drove the placement of district lines.

For your worksheet, find the problem, the voters’ claim, what the Court decided, and what it did not decide. Write a short answer for each—not the whole paragraph.

What happened? Why did voters sue? What did the Court decide? What question did the Court leave open?

What happened?

North Carolina drew a long, narrow District 12 that linked Black neighborhoods across several counties.

After the 1990 census, the state gained an additional U.S. House seat, bringing its total to 12. The Justice Department objected to its first map under the Voting Rights Act, a law protecting voting rights against racial discrimination.

The state wanted Black voters to have a better chance to elect someone they supported. Black voters made up more than half of District 12. That made it a MAJORITY-MINORITY DISTRICT.

Why did voters sue?

The voters said the state sorted people into a district mainly by race. They argued that this violated equal protection.

The Equal Protection Clause is the part of the Fourteenth Amendment requiring states to treat people equally under the law. Ruth Shaw and four other voters argued that race mattered more than ordinary mapmaking rules, such as keeping communities together.

What did the Court decide?

The Court ruled 5–4 that the voters’ challenge could continue. It sent the case back to a lower court.

If race was the main reason for the lines, judges would ask two hard questions:

  • Did the state have a very strong reason?
  • Did it use race only as much as needed?

This demanding test is called STRICT SCRUTINY. In legal terms, the map must be narrowly tailored to a compelling governmental interest.

What question did the Court leave open?

The Court let the challenge continue. It did not decide in 1993 that District 12 was unconstitutional.

The lower court still had to evaluate the allegations and the state’s justification. Applying strict scrutiny does not automatically invalidate a district, and the ruling did not ban every majority-minority district.

Read more about the case

North Carolina drew District 12 after the U.S. Department of Justice objected to its first map under the Voting Rights Act. Justice Byron White dissented, meaning he disagreed with the ruling. He argued that the voters had not shown that the district reduced their ability to participate or elect candidates.
