Shaw v. Reno (1993)

Voters can challenge a map under the Fourteenth Amendment when they allege that race drove the placement of district lines.

For your worksheet, find the problem, the voters’ claim, what the Court decided, and what it did not decide. Write a short answer for each—not the whole paragraph.

What happened? Why did voters sue? What did the Court decide? What question did the Court leave open? After the worksheet

What happened?

After the 1990 census, North Carolina gained an additional U.S. House seat, bringing its total to 12. The state drew a new map. District 12 stretched about 160 miles, linking Black neighborhoods across several counties. Voters challenged how race shaped its boundaries. It was a majority-minority district, where a racial or ethnic minority group makes up more than half the population.

Why did voters sue?

Ruth Shaw and four other voters argued that North Carolina had drawn the district mainly to group voters by race. They sued under the Equal Protection Clause, the part of the Fourteenth Amendment that requires states to treat people equally under the law.

What did the Court decide?

The Supreme Court ruled 5–4 that the voters’ Equal Protection claim could go forward. It sent the case back to a lower court for further review.

What question did the Court leave open?

The Court left District 12’s constitutionality unresolved in 1993. A district’s shape can be evidence that race drove the boundaries. When race takes priority over ordinary districting criteria, such as keeping towns together, courts apply strict scrutiny.

Under this demanding test, the state must show that it had a compelling reason to use race and drew the lines closely enough to serve that reason. A district can survive strict scrutiny if it meets both requirements.

After the worksheet: try another case

A commission draws a compact congressional district with a majority of voters from one racial group. Its records show that it split several towns to reach a racial target.

Read this second case. In Bush v. Vera (1996), Texas used detailed racial data to draw congressional districts and departed from ordinary criteria such as keeping districts compact. The Supreme Court upheld the judgment that three challenged districts failed strict scrutiny under Equal Protection.

Show a sample response

District 12’s long, narrow stretches raise questions about what shaped the lines. Mapmaking records can show whether race took priority over other criteria.

Shaw v. Reno applies because the records suggest race took priority over ordinary districting criteria. Both cases involve Equal Protection. Shaw allowed a claim to proceed, while Bush upheld invalidation of the challenged Texas districts. If race predominates, the state must show a compelling interest and a map narrowly tailored to that interest.

Read more about the case

North Carolina drew District 12 after the U.S. Department of Justice objected to its first map under the Voting Rights Act. Justice Byron White dissented, meaning he disagreed with the ruling. He argued that the voters had not shown that the district reduced their ability to participate or elect candidates.

For claims about maps drawn to favor a political party, Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) held that federal courts cannot decide partisan gerrymandering claims under the federal Constitution. Challenges under state constitutions may still go to state courts.
