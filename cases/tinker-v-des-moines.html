Unit 3

Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District (1969)

Public school students have speech rights. Schools can limit expression that substantially disrupts school or infringes on others’ rights.

What happened?What was the constitutional issue?What did the Court decide?What are the decision’s limits?

What happened?

Des Moines students wore black armbands to school to protest the Vietnam War, and school officials suspended them.

The students included John Tinker, Mary Beth Tinker, and Christopher Eckhardt. Officials had adopted a rule banning the armbands before students wore them. The families challenged the suspensions, arguing that the silent protest was protected by the First Amendment.

What was the constitutional issue?

Could the school ban this political expression without evidence of serious interference with school or other students’ rights?

An action that communicates a message, such as an armband, is symbolic speech. A substantial disruption is a serious interference with school activities. Officials feared disagreement and discomfort, but the case required more than a general fear of an unpopular opinion.

What did the Court decide?

The Court held that punishing these students for wearing the armbands violated their freedom of speech.

The protest was quiet, and officials lacked evidence supporting a reasonable prediction of substantial disruption or interference with others’ rights. Students do not lose First Amendment protections simply because they enter a public school. Disagreement with the students’ political message did not justify the ban.

Abe Fortas · decision, 7–2

What are the decision’s limits?

Tinker protects non-disruptive student expression like these armbands; it does not make every kind of student speech immune from school rules.

Under Tinker, officials need specific grounds to reasonably forecast substantial disruption or an invasion of others’ rights. Separate rules apply to some other settings, such as school-sponsored expression or lewd speech at school. Do not turn this holding into an absolute rule for every message, location, or school activity.
