BRUTUS · IDENTITY UNCERTAIN

PARTICIPATORY DEMOCRACY

In PARTICIPATORY DEMOCRACY, ordinary people take an active part in government. This POLITICAL PARTICIPATION includes speaking at meetings, contacting officials, protesting, organizing, and voting. Sometimes citizens use DIRECT DEMOCRACY, which means they vote on a law or policy themselves instead of leaving the decision to representatives.

WHAT THAT MEANS Government should hear directly from many people before it acts. For the social-media decision, Congress would ask the public for ideas and comments instead of listening mostly to powerful groups or experts.

WHOSE VOICE GETS HEARD? People who vote, attend meetings, contact officials, protest, or otherwise take part can speak directly to government.

WHOSE VOICE MAY BE MISSED? People who have less time, access, or confidence to participate may be heard less often.

SEE THE CONNECTION IN BRUTUS NO. 1

Brutus worried that representatives in a very large country would be too far away to understand what ordinary people need. That connects to participatory democracy because both stress keeping government close to the people. Brutus did not use the modern term “participatory democracy,” but his warning reflects the same concern.

JAMES MADISON

PLURALIST DEMOCRACY

In PLURALIST DEMOCRACY, people gain influence by joining groups built around shared concerns. A FACTION is a group of people united by the same interest or goal. An INTEREST GROUP is an organized group that tries to influence government policy. Groups may use LOBBYING—contacting public officials to persuade them—or form a COALITION, meaning several groups working together. The final policy may be a COMPROMISE, a decision in which each side gets some, but not all, of what it wants.

WHAT THAT MEANS One person can be easy to ignore, but an organized group is harder to ignore. For the social-media decision, Congress would hear from technology companies, parents’ groups, free-speech groups, child-safety groups, and others.

WHOSE VOICE GETS HEARD? People represented by organized groups can combine their voices and push government to pay attention.

WHOSE VOICE MAY BE MISSED? People whose concerns do not have a large, well-funded, or well-connected group may have less influence.

SEE THE CONNECTION IN FEDERALIST NO. 10

Madison says factions will always exist because people want different things. In a large republic, many factions compete, which makes it harder for one group to control everything. That is the link to pluralist democracy.

ALEXANDER HAMILTON

ELITE DEMOCRACY

In ELITE DEMOCRACY, a relatively small group has the most influence over government decisions. These POLITICAL ELITES may include elected leaders, experts, wealthy people, and people with strong connections. Citizens still elect representatives, but leaders and experts do most of the work of writing and deciding policy. An INSTITUTIONAL FILTER is a part of government—such as Congress or the courts—that stands between what the public wants right now and the final decision.

WHAT THAT MEANS Most people do not help write each law themselves. For the social-media decision, members of Congress and experts would study the issue, hear testimony, and make the final choice.

WHOSE VOICE GETS HEARD? Elected officials, experts, wealthy people, and well-connected people have the most direct influence over the decision.

WHOSE VOICE MAY BE MISSED? Ordinary people may be overlooked when decision-makers do not hear or share their experiences.

SEE THE CONNECTION IN THE U.S. CONSTITUTION

The Constitution says government gets its power from the people, and voters elect members of the House. But citizens do not vote directly on national laws. Instead, the House, Senate, president, and courts each have different jobs. Public wishes must pass through these parts of government before becoming policy. This creates a connection to elite democracy because elected leaders and other officials make the final decisions.
