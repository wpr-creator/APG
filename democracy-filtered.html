START WITH THE IDEA

REPUBLICANISM

People govern through elected representatives. Representation means those officials speak and act for people who cannot all make national policy directly.

PEOPLE'S VOICES→REPRESENTATIVES→PUBLIC POLICY

ESSENTIAL QUESTION: How does representative democracy decide whose voices matter?

THE SCENARIO Congress is considering new rules and regulations for social-media platforms. Millions of people have opinions, organizations are pushing competing plans, and elected officials must decide what to do.

THE DECISION

WHO SHOULD GOVERNMENT LISTEN TO?

When making a new social-media policy, who should government listen to most?

TEST THE SYSTEM

THREE DEMOCRATIC FILTERS

0 OF 3 FILTERS TESTED

WHY THESE THREE? In the social-media scenario, Congress cannot listen equally to millions of individual voices, every organization, and every expert. These three models show three different ways elected representatives could decide whose voices carry the most influence.

  1. Participatory: Listen broadly to individual citizens.
  2. Pluralist: Weigh organizations competing over the policy.
  3. Elite / filtered: Trust elected leaders and experts to decide.

YOUR JOB: Test each route from public voice to government policy. Notice who becomes louder, who may be missed, and which model you think Congress should use.

POSSIBLY ROBERT YATES

PARTICIPATORY DEMOCRACY

“KEEP GOVERNMENT CLOSE.”

Participatory democracy says democracy works best when many ordinary people take an active role—not only by choosing representatives, but by speaking, organizing, voting, protesting, attending meetings, and sometimes deciding policies directly.

IN PLAIN LANGUAGE The more directly citizens can make government hear and respond to them, the more democratic the system is. For the social-media decision, Congress would collect broad public input rather than listening mainly to powerful organizations or experts.

PEOPLE→DIRECT ACTION→GOVERNMENT
LOOK FOR IT TODAYTown hallsProtestsPrimariesBallot initiatives

AMPLIFIES Broad citizen participation

CAN MISS People with less time, access, or political confidence

SEE IT IN BRUTUS NO. 1

Brutus fears that a very large republic will make representatives too distant to know what ordinary people need. That warning supports the participatory goal of keeping government close and responsive—but Brutus is criticizing the proposed Constitution, not naming a modern model of democracy.

FEDERALIST NO. 10

PLURALIST DEMOCRACY

“LET INTERESTS COMPETE.”

Pluralist democracy says people gain influence by joining organizations built around shared interests. Many groups compete for attention, negotiate, form coalitions, and pressure officials, so public policy often emerges through compromise.

IN PLAIN LANGUAGE One person may be easy to ignore; an organized group is harder to ignore. For the social-media decision, Congress would weigh competing arguments from technology companies, parents, civil-liberties organizations, child-safety advocates, and other groups.

PEOPLE→GROUPS COMPETE→GOVERNMENT
LOOK FOR IT TODAYInterest groupsUnionsCoalitionsLobbying

AMPLIFIES Organized interests and coalitions

CAN MISS Interests without equal money, membership, or access

SEE IT IN FEDERALIST NO. 10

Madison says factions are unavoidable because people have different interests. A large republic contains many competing factions, making it harder for one group to dominate. He also expects elected representatives to refine public views, so Federalist No. 10 supports both group competition and institutional filtering.

INSTITUTIONAL FILTERING

ELITE / FILTERED DEMOCRACY

“LET INSTITUTIONS REFINE.”

Elite democracy emphasizes the influence of elected leaders, experts, wealthy or well-connected people, and durable institutions. Instead of converting every public demand directly into policy, the system filters those demands through representatives and government structures.

IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Citizens choose leaders, but leaders and experts do most of the detailed policymaking. For social-media policy, congressional committees would study evidence, hear specialized testimony, negotiate, and make the final decision.

PEOPLE→LEADERS + EXPERTS→POLICY
LOOK FOR IT TODAYCommitteesAgenciesCourtsTrustee representation

AMPLIFIES Expertise, deliberation, and institutional stability

CAN MISS Ordinary people far from powerful decision-makers

SEE IT IN THE U.S. CONSTITUTION

The Constitution begins with popular authority and creates an elected House, but it does not use direct national voting on laws. The Senate, Electoral College, federal courts, separate branches, and different terms filter public demands through institutions. Its design combines popular participation with elite or filtered features.
