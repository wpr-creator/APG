REPUBLICANISM

People govern through elected representatives. Representation means those officials speak and act for people who cannot all make national policy directly.

PEOPLE'S VOICES→REPRESENTATIVES→PUBLIC POLICY

ESSENTIAL QUESTION: How does representative democracy decide whose voices matter?

THREE DEMOCRATIC FILTERS

0 OF 3 FILTERS TESTED

WHY THESE THREE? Congress cannot listen equally to millions of people, every organization, and every expert. These three models offer different answers to one question: Who should have the most influence when representatives make a decision?

  1. Participatory: Listen to as many individual people as possible.
  2. Pluralist: Listen to organized groups that compete to be heard.
  3. Elite: Let elected leaders and experts make the decision.

YOUR JOB: Explore each model. Ask who gets heard, who might be left out, and which model Congress should use for the social-media policy.

BRUTUS · IDENTITY UNCERTAIN

PARTICIPATORY DEMOCRACY

“LET THE PEOPLE TAKE PART.”

In participatory democracy, ordinary people take an active part in government. They do more than elect representatives. They also speak at meetings, contact officials, protest, organize, vote, and sometimes vote directly on a law or policy.

WHAT THAT MEANS Government should hear directly from many people before it acts. For the social-media decision, Congress would ask the public for ideas and comments instead of listening mostly to powerful groups or experts.

PEOPLE→DIRECT ACTION→GOVERNMENT
LOOK FOR IT TODAYTown hallsProtestsPrimariesBallot initiatives

WHO GETS MORE POWER Ordinary people who take part

WHO MAY BE MISSED People who lack time, access, or confidence to speak up

SEE THE CONNECTION IN BRUTUS NO. 1

Brutus worried that representatives in a very large country would be too far away to understand what ordinary people need. That connects to participatory democracy because both stress keeping government close to the people. Brutus did not use the modern term “participatory democracy,” but his warning reflects the same concern.

JAMES MADISON

PLURALIST DEMOCRACY

“LET GROUPS COMPETE.”

In pluralist democracy, people join groups built around shared concerns. Those groups compete to persuade government. Officials hear different sides, and the final policy may be a compromise—a decision in which each side gets some, but not all, of what it wants.

WHAT THAT MEANS One person can be easy to ignore, but an organized group is harder to ignore. For the social-media decision, Congress would hear from technology companies, parents’ groups, free-speech groups, child-safety groups, and others.

PEOPLE→GROUPS COMPETE→GOVERNMENT
LOOK FOR IT TODAYInterest groupsUnionsGroups working togetherGroups contacting lawmakers

WHO GETS MORE POWER People who organize into groups

WHO MAY BE MISSED Groups with less money, fewer members, or less access to officials

SEE THE CONNECTION IN FEDERALIST NO. 10

Madison calls a group united by a shared interest a faction. He says factions will always exist because people want different things. In a large republic, many factions compete, which makes it harder for one group to control everything. That is the link to pluralist democracy.

ALEXANDER HAMILTON

ELITE DEMOCRACY

“LET LEADERS AND EXPERTS DECIDE.”

In elite democracy, elected leaders, experts, wealthy people, and well-connected people have the most influence over government decisions. Citizens choose representatives, but a much smaller group does most of the work of writing and deciding policy.

WHAT THAT MEANS Most people do not help write each law themselves. For the social-media decision, members of Congress and experts would study the issue, hear testimony, and make the final choice.

PEOPLE ELECT LEADERS→LEADERS + EXPERTS DECIDE→POLICY
LOOK FOR IT TODAYCongressional committeesGovernment agenciesCourtsExpert testimony

WHO GETS MORE POWER Leaders, experts, and people with strong connections

WHO MAY BE MISSED Ordinary people who are far from the decision-makers

SEE THE CONNECTION IN THE U.S. CONSTITUTION

The Constitution says government gets its power from the people, and voters elect members of the House. But citizens do not vote directly on national laws. Instead, the House, Senate, president, and courts each have different jobs. Public wishes must pass through these parts of government before becoming policy. This creates a connection to elite democracy because elected leaders and other officials make the final decisions.

THREE DOCUMENTS, THREE CLUES

These documents do not simply name a model. Each gives you evidence about how representation should work. Ask what problem the author sees and what solution the document supports.

01

BRUTUS NO. 1

THE WORRY: A large national republic may become too distant for representatives to know and express the people’s views.

THE CONNECTION: Supports keeping participation close to ordinary citizens and warns that power can settle into the hands of a few.

02

FEDERALIST NO. 10

THE WORRY: Factions may sacrifice the rights of others or the public good to their own interests.

THE CONNECTION: A large republic multiplies competing interests, while elected representatives can refine public views.

03

U.S. CONSTITUTION

THE DESIGN: Citizens elect representatives, but the Constitution divides power among offices and branches. Public wishes must pass through those parts of government before becoming policy.

THE CONNECTION: The House connects government to voters. The rest of the system gives leaders time to study and debate decisions so government can remain stable.
