Division of Powers

Federalism divides governing authority between the national government and the states. Some powers belong mainly to one level; others belong to both.

How Is Power Divided?

Read each side, then examine the powers in the overlap.

National Government

Enumerated Powers

Powers specifically listed in the Constitution.

  • Regulate interstate commerce
  • Coin money
  • Establish post offices
  • Raise and support armies
Implied Powers

Powers not specifically listed that grow from enumerated powers through the NECESSARY AND PROPER—or ELASTIC—CLAUSE.

Both

Concurrent Powers
  • Collect taxes
  • Borrow money
  • Establish courts
  • Make and enforce laws

Both levels may exercise these powers.

State Governments

Reserved Powers

Powers kept by the states under the Tenth Amendment.

  • Run elections
  • Provide public education
  • License professions
  • Manage local policing

When Powers Conflict · Supremacy Clause

A valid federal law takes precedence over a conflicting state law.

Federal law is supreme only when the national government is exercising a constitutional power.

KEY: Enumerated and implied powers belong to the national government. Reserved powers belong to the states. Concurrent powers belong to both.
