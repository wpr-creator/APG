Articles of Confederation

Continental Congress · Drafted 1777 · Ratified March 1, 1781 · Replaced 1789
Historical Context
The Articles of Confederation were the United States’ first constitution, in force from 1781 to 1789. They created a union in which states retained substantial authority and Congress depended on them for money and enforcement. Difficulties with debt, trade, and unrest helped build support for the Constitutional Convention. The Confederation also made important decisions, including the Northwest Ordinance.

How the government was organized

Each state retains its sovereignty, freedom, and independence, and every power, jurisdiction, and right, which is not by this Confederation expressly delegated to the United States, in Congress assembled. (Article II)
The states kept powers not expressly given to the United States. Congress depended heavily on state governments to carry out national decisions.
The United States in Congress assembled shall have authority... to build and equip a navy; to agree upon the number of land forces, and to make requisitions from each state for its quota... but in determining these questions... the vote of nine States shall be necessary. (Article IX)
Article IX required nine states for specified major decisions. Other questions generally required a majority of the states. Changes to the Articles required Congress’s agreement and approval by every state legislature.

What Congress could do

Congress under the Articles could: declare war and make peace, send and receive ambassadors, enter into treaties, coin money, run a postal service, manage relations with Native American tribes, and govern the territory northwest of the Ohio River (the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 was passed under the Articles — one of its genuine successes).
The Confederation also had achievements: the Treaty of Paris ended the Revolutionary War, and the Northwest Ordinance established a process for admitting states and prohibited slavery in the Northwest Territory.

Where the system struggled

Requesting money from states
Congress could request money but could not tax people directly. Unpaid state contributions made national debts and expenses difficult to meet.
Constitution Fix: Article I, Section 8 — Congress has power to tax
Regulating trade
Congress lacked a general power to regulate interstate and foreign commerce. States adopted conflicting trade rules.
Constitution Fix: Commerce Clause — Congress regulates interstate commerce
Carrying out national decisions
There was no separate national executive branch. Congress depended heavily on state officials to carry out decisions.
Constitution Fix: Article II creates the executive branch
Resolving legal disputes
There was no general federal judiciary like the later Article III system. The Articles did provide limited ways to resolve specified disputes, including disputes between states.
Constitution Fix: Article III creates the federal judiciary
Supplying an army
Congress could decide to raise forces, but depended on states to supply troops and money. Those dependencies weakened its ability to respond.
Constitution Fix: Congress has power to raise armies; President is commander-in-chief
Changing the Articles
Amendments required Congress’s agreement and approval by every state legislature. A single state could block a proposed change.
Constitution Fix: Article V — 3/4 of states needed to amend

Why Shays’ Rebellion mattered

In 1786–87, Massachusetts farmers facing debts and property losses protested and closed courts. Armed resistance associated with Daniel Shays increased fears about disorder. The national government struggled to fund and organize an effective response. Massachusetts forces, supported by private funding, suppressed the uprising.
Shays’ Rebellion increased concern about the national government’s ability to respond to unrest. It helped strengthen support for changing the governing system.
