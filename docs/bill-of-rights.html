Historical Context — Read This First

Anti-Federalists objected that the original Constitution did not explicitly protect individual liberties. Federalists promised amendments as a condition of ratification, and James Madison guided them through the First Congress. Originally these limits applied only to the federal government; after the Fourteenth Amendment, the Supreme Court gradually applied most of them to the states through selective incorporation.

The Ten Amendments

First Amendment

Religion, speech, press, assembly, and petition

Protects five core freedoms. The Establishment Clause bars government sponsorship of religion; the Free Exercise Clause protects religious practice. Speech and press protections are broad but not absolute.

Engel v. VitaleSchenck v. United StatesTinker v. Des MoinesNew York Times v. United States
Second Amendment

Right to keep and bear arms

Protects an individual right to possess firearms. The Supreme Court incorporated this protection against state and local governments in McDonald v. Chicago.

McDonald v. Chicago
Third Amendment

No forced quartering of soldiers

Government generally may not require people to house soldiers in their homes, reflecting the founders' concern about military intrusion into private life.

Fourth Amendment

Protection from unreasonable searches and seizures

Usually requires government searches to be supported by probable cause and a particularized warrant. Evidence gathered unlawfully may be excluded from trial.

Fifth Amendment

Rights of the accused and due process

Protects against self-incrimination and double jeopardy, requires due process, provides grand-jury protection in federal cases, and requires just compensation when government takes private property.

Sixth Amendment

Fair and speedy criminal trial

Guarantees a speedy and public trial, an impartial jury, notice of charges, confrontation of witnesses, compulsory process, and assistance of counsel.

Gideon v. Wainwright
Seventh Amendment

Jury trial in federal civil cases

Preserves the right to a jury in many federal civil lawsuits and limits courts from reexamining a jury's factual findings.

Eighth Amendment

Limits on punishment

Prohibits excessive bail, excessive fines, and cruel and unusual punishment.

Ninth Amendment

People retain unlisted rights

Makes clear that listing certain rights in the Constitution does not mean the people possess only those enumerated rights.

Tenth Amendment

Reserved powers

Powers not delegated to the United States and not prohibited to the states are reserved to the states or the people, reinforcing federalism.
