Brutus No. 1

Brutus (pen name; authorship disputed) · October 18, 1787 · New York Journal
Historical Context
Brutus No. 1 appeared on October 18, 1787, before the Federalist essays began. Its author used the pen name Brutus; the author’s identity is debated. Brutus opposed the proposed Constitution. He feared that a large national republic and broad federal powers would weaken state governments and put liberty at risk. Read the passages as his arguments against ratification, alongside Madison’s arguments in favor.

Why Brutus feared federal power

This government is to possess absolute and uncontroulable power, legislative, executive and judicial, with respect to every object to which it extends, for by the last clause of section 8th, article 1st, it is declared "that the Congress shall have power to make all laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into execution the foregoing powers, and all other powers vested by this constitution, in the government of the United States."
Brutus feared that Congress would use the Necessary and Proper Clause to expand its authority. That is his warning about the proposed system, not a rule that Congress has unlimited power.
It is true this government is limited to certain objects, or to speak more properly, some small degree of power is still left to the states, but a little attention to the powers vested in the general government, will convince every candid man, that if it is capable of being executed, all that is reserved for the individual states must very shortly be annihilated.
Brutus predicted that federal power would gradually displace state power. Identify the danger he feared without treating that prediction as an established outcome.

Why Brutus favored a small republic

History furnishes no example of a free republic, any thing like the extent of the United States. The Grecian republics were of small extent; so also was that of the Romans. Both of these, it is true, in process of time, extended their conquests over large territories of country; and the consequence was, that their governments were changed from that of free governments to those of the most tyrannical that ever existed in the world.
Brutus used Greek and Roman history to argue that a large republic could not remain free. Madison’s defense of a large republic offers a competing argument.
In a large republic, the public good is sacrificed to a thousand views; it is subordinate to exceptions, and depends on accidents. In a small one, the interest of the public is easier perceived, better understood, and more within the reach of every citizen.
Brutus quotes Montesquieu here. He uses this argument to support his concern that a large republic would lose touch with the people it represented.

Why Brutus feared for individual rights

The powers of the general legislature extend to every case that is of the least importance — there is nothing valuable to human nature, nothing dear to freemen, but what is within its power. It has authority to make laws which will affect the lives, the liberty, and property of every man in the United States; nor can the constitution or laws of any state, in any way prevent or impede the full and complete execution of every power given.
Brutus worried that federal supremacy would leave states unable to resist an expanding national government. The Constitution makes valid federal law supreme; it does not make every federal action constitutional.

The later Bill of Rights

Course connection: Brutus’s warning about concentrated power belongs to the broader Anti-Federalist concern that the proposed Constitution needed clearer safeguards for individual rights. Congress proposed amendments in 1789, and the states ratified the Bill of Rights in 1791.
This is historical context, not a quotation from Brutus No. 1. It connects the concern about government power to the later addition of explicit rights protections.
