Emancipation Proclamation

President Abraham Lincoln · Executive Order · January 1, 1863
Historical Context
During the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln issued a preliminary emancipation proclamation in September 1862 and the final proclamation on January 1, 1863. Acting as commander in chief, he declared freedom for enslaved people in designated areas in rebellion. Its reach was limited, and enforcement depended on Union military success. Read both what it declared and whom it excluded.

The authority Lincoln used

Now, therefore I, Abraham Lincoln, President of the United States, by virtue of the power in me vested as Commander-in-Chief, of the Army and Navy of the United States in time of actual armed rebellion against the authority and government of the United States, and as a fit and necessary war measure for suppressing said rebellion, do, on this first day of January, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and sixty-three, order and declare that all persons held as slaves within said designated States, and parts of States, are, and henceforward shall be free.
Lincoln relied on his commander-in-chief authority during rebellion. That legal basis explains the proclamation’s wartime scope; it does not mean the action lacked a moral purpose.

What the proclamation changed

The Proclamation freed enslaved people in the Confederate states — specifically the states "in rebellion" against the United States. It also authorized the enlistment of formerly enslaved men into the Union Army and Navy. By the end of the war, approximately 180,000 Black men had served in the Union Army, comprising about 10% of its total strength. The Proclamation also fundamentally changed the character of the war — the proclamation increased the political cost of European support for the Confederacy.
The proclamation declared freedom in designated areas in rebellion and authorized Black military service. Enforcing freedom depended on Union advances.

Whom it did not free

The Proclamation applied only to Confederate states in rebellion. It explicitly exempted enslaved people in Union border states (Kentucky, Missouri, Maryland, Delaware) and in Confederate areas already under Union control. It also had no mechanism for enforcement in Confederate territory where Union forces had no presence. It did not grant citizenship to freed people, did not address the legal status of slavery in the United States generally, and was not a constitutional amendment — it relied on wartime executive authority rather than permanently changing the Constitution.
The proclamation did not end slavery throughout the country. The Thirteenth Amendment later abolished slavery nationally, except as punishment for crime after conviction.
Specifically exempted: Tennessee (largely under Union control), parts of Louisiana including New Orleans, and the 48 counties of western Virginia that would become West Virginia. The border states — Missouri, Kentucky, Maryland, Delaware — which had not seceded and remained in the Union, were also entirely exempt. Enslaved people in these areas were not freed by the Proclamation.
The border states and listed exceptions were outside the proclamation’s reach. Its military legal basis and Lincoln’s efforts to preserve Union support shaped these limits.

Why it mattered

The Emancipation Proclamation transformed the Civil War from a war to preserve the Union into a war to end slavery — whether Lincoln intended it that way or not. Frederick Douglass, initially skeptical, recognized its power: "It is not a proclamation of 'liberty throughout all the land, unto all the inhabitants thereof,' such as we had hoped for, but it is a proclamation of freedom to the slaves of the rebels in arms." The Thirteenth Amendment would place nationwide abolition in the Constitution rather than rely on a wartime proclamation.
Douglass welcomed emancipation while recognizing its limits. The proclamation was an important step toward abolition, not the completion of equal rights.
