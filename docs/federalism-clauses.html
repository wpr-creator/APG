The Constitutional Boundary

The Constitution gives the national government real power, keeps other powers with the states, and establishes which law controls when the two levels collide.

Enumerated PowersImplied PowersFederal SupremacyReserved Powers
FEDERALISM is not a clean split. These four constitutional rules create a continuing argument over where national power ends and state power begins.
Article I, Section 8 · Enumerated Power

The Commerce Clause

Congress shall have Power “to regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian Tribes.”
In plain language

Congress may regulate trade and economic activity that crosses state lines. The argument is usually about how closely an activity must connect to interstate commerce.

Case connection

United States v. Lopez ruled that carrying a gun near a school was too far removed from interstate commerce.

Article I, Section 8 · Implied Power

The Necessary and Proper Clause

Congress may “make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution” its listed powers.
In plain language

Congress is not limited to actions named word for word. It may choose reasonable ways to carry out its enumerated powers. These are called IMPLIED POWERS.

Case connection

McCulloch v. Maryland used this clause to uphold Congress’s power to create a national bank.

Article VI · Conflict Between Governments

The Supremacy Clause

The Constitution and valid laws of the United States “shall be the supreme Law of the Land.”
In plain language

When a valid federal law conflicts with a state law, the federal law wins. Federal power must still come from the Constitution.

Case connection

McCulloch v. Maryland ruled that Maryland could not tax the federal bank because a state may not control or destroy a valid federal action.

Tenth Amendment · State and Local Authority

Reserved Powers

“The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.”
In plain language

If the Constitution does not give a power to the national government or forbid it to the states, that power stays with the states or the people.

Common examples

States lead in areas such as local policing, public education, professional licensing, and elections—unless a valid federal power applies.

McCulloch v. Maryland

Necessary and Proper + Supremacy: national power can be broad when Congress pursues a constitutional goal.

The lesson in one line

The national government has enumerated and implied powers—but it does not have every power.
