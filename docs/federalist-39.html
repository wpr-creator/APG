Unit 1

Federalist No. 39

James Madison · Publius · January 16, 1788
Historical Context
In January 1788, Americans were debating whether to approve the proposed Constitution. Madison addressed two questions: Is the new government a republic? Is it federal, national, or a mixture? In this essay, “federal” emphasizes the states as political units; “national” emphasizes the people as one nation.

Federalism divides governing authority between national and state governments. This division limits concentrated power and gives people several access points—places to seek government action. A group might petition a state legislature or members of Congress.

Federal and national features

In Madison’s argument, federal features preserve the states as political units; national features concern the people as one nation.

What Madison meant by a republic

Madison begins by defining the term at stake: "a government which derives all its powers directly or indirectly from the great body of the people, and is administered by persons holding their offices during pleasure, for a limited period, or during good behavior." Any government meeting this definition is a republic — size and structural details are secondary.
Madison defines a republic by authority derived from the people and officials serving on the terms he lists—including judges serving during good behavior. He does not require every official to be directly elected or to serve a short term.
Madison surveys existing state governments and finds every one already meets his definition of a republic — mixed features, indirect elections, appointed officials. The new Constitution, he argues, follows the same pattern rather than departing from it. The republican principle is "not less applicable to a large than to a small republic."
Madison points to state governments to show that indirect selection and different lengths of service can still fit his definition of a republic.

How the Constitution combines states and people

"The Constitution is, in strictness, neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both." Madison examines five features: its foundation in state ratification (federal); the sources of officials’ authority (mixed); its direct operation on individuals (national); the extent of its limited powers (federal); and its amendment process (mixed). Presidential selection combines state and population-based features.
Madison argues that the Constitution combines federal features, organized through states, with national features, organized around the people as one nation.
On the foundation: the Constitution is ratified by the people of each state — acting as states, not as one national people. "It is to be the assent and ratification of the several States, derived from the supreme authority in each State — the authority of the people themselves." This makes the founding act federal, not national.
Approval through separate state conventions makes ratification federal in Madison’s classification. It was not one nationwide popular vote.
On the scope of power: "The difference between a federal and national government, as it relates to the operation of the government, is supposed to consist in this, that in the former the powers operate on the political bodies composing the confederacy... in the latter, on the individual citizens composing the nation." Madison classifies the operation of the new government’s powers as national because they act directly on individuals. He separately classifies the extent of its limited powers as federal.
National power operates directly on individuals, rather than only through requests to state governments. Madison distinguishes how power operates from how far it extends.

Why the amendment process is mixed

Madison's last piece of evidence: the amendment process requires ratification by three-fourths of the states — not a simple national majority, and not unanimity (as required under the Articles). This is federal in character because the states as units must approve changes, but the unanimity requirement is dropped, making it more national than the Articles.
Amendments need broad agreement among states, but not unanimous approval by every state. Madison therefore classifies the amendment process as neither wholly federal nor wholly national.
