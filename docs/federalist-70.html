Federalist No. 70

Energy, unity, and accountability: Hamilton’s argument for one president who can act—and answer for the result.

Author and setting

Alexander Hamilton wrote Federalist No. 70 as Publius during the debate over approving the Constitution. Critics feared that a powerful president could threaten liberty.

Hamilton argued that one president could lead effectively and be held responsible. His central question was why one person, rather than several executives, should lead the executive branch.

The meaning and purposes of energy

Energy means the ability to make decisions and carry them out effectively. Hamilton argued that an energetic executive was necessary for good government.

“Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government.”Alexander Hamilton · Federalist No. 70

Why energy matters

  • National defense — Respond when the country faces attack.
  • Enforcing laws — Make sure federal laws are carried out.
  • Protecting property — Protect people’s possessions and property rights.
  • Securing liberty — Protect people’s freedom and rights.

Four advantages of unity

Hamilton argued that one executive could act more effectively than several executives who had to agree.

Accountability and two safeguards

Accountability means being answerable for official actions. Hamilton argued that one executive makes responsibility easier to identify.

Several executives

Hypothetical: Several executives disagree about travel restrictions. Each could blame the others for the result.

One president

With one president directing the response, responsibility is easier to identify.

Two safeguards for republican government

  • Dependence on the people — Elections give the public a recurring voice in who leads.
  • Responsibility for official conduct — The president’s actions can be questioned, investigated, and judged.

Poor judgment can bring public criticism and electoral consequences. Serious misconduct can lead to impeachment by the House—a formal accusation—and a trial in the Senate.

Additional constitutional checks

Elections: voters influence who holds office. Impeachment: the House accuses; the Senate tries the case and can remove after conviction.

Funding: Congress controls federal spending. Confirmation: the Senate approves specified presidential appointments.

These are constitutional checks for context, separate from Hamilton’s two safeguards above.

Lesson EQ connection

How can a president lead decisively without opening the door to tyranny?

In Federalist No. 70, Hamilton argued that one president could lead decisively through clear direction and sufficient powers. Dependence on the people and responsibility for official conduct help guard against opening the door to tyranny.

AP exam focus
  • Identify Hamilton’s argument for one executive.
  • Explain how unity supports effective action.
  • Explain how identifiable responsibility supports accountability.
Sources and optional clarification
  • Library of Congress: Federalist No. 70. Hamilton’s essay appeared in the New York Packet on March 18, 1788. The quotation above follows this transcription; the explanations are paraphrases.
  • U.S. Constitution, Articles I and II: elections, impeachment, the Senate trial, and shared powers. Senate conviction requires two-thirds of senators present.
Optional: unity, modern theory, and historical comparisons

Hamilton’s defense of a single executive does not by itself settle modern unitary executive theory—debates about presidential control over executive officials. Unity also does not guarantee good decisions or make the president personally responsible for every outcome.

Hamilton discusses Britain’s king and council to argue that advisers may help identify responsibility where a monarch is legally protected, but may obscure responsibility around an already accountable republican executive. This is his historical argument, not a description of every monarchy.
