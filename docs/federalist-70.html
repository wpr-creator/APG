Energy, unity, and accountability: Hamilton’s argument for one president who can act—and answer for the result.

Document background

Alexander Hamilton wrote Federalist No. 70 under the name Publius during the debate over ratifying the Constitution—approving it as the new plan of government. It appeared in the New York Packet on March 18, 1788.

Critics worried that a powerful presidency could threaten liberty. Hamilton addressed a central design question: Why should one person, rather than several executives or a controlling council, lead the executive branch? He defended a single president who could act effectively and be held responsible.

Energy in the executive

Hamilton’s central argument is that an energetic executive is necessary for effective government. Here, energy means the capacity to decide and carry out responsibilities effectively—not unlimited power or personal enthusiasm.

“Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government.”Alexander Hamilton · Federalist No. 70 · Read the original

Why energy matters

  • Defense against foreign attacks

    The government needs direction and the ability to respond when the country is threatened.

  • Steady administration of laws

    Laws need consistent enforcement; passing them is not enough.

  • Protection of property

    Government must protect property against groups that disrupt justice or use force to take advantage of others.

  • Security of liberty

    Government must protect freedom against ambitious leaders, factions pursuing their own advantage, and a breakdown of public order.

Four ingredients of executive energy

Hamilton’s terms and what they mean
IngredientStudent explanation
UnityOne executive provides direction and makes responsibility easier to identify.
DurationTime in office supports follow-through on a plan.
Adequate supportReliable financial support sustains the office and helps preserve its independence. Hamilton calls this an adequate provision for its support.
Competent powersSufficient legal authority enables the president to perform the job. Competent means suited to the responsibility—not permission to do anything.

These four ingredients describe capacity to act. Hamilton separately asks what makes that power safe in a republic.

Unity: why one executive?

Hamilton argues that one executive generally makes decision, activity, secrecy, and dispatch easier than shared executive authority does. These are advantages he claims for the design, not guarantees that every president will make good choices.

Four advantages Hamilton associates with unity
DecisionReaching a clear choice.
ActivityActing on the choice rather than leaving it unfinished.
SecrecyKeeping sensitive plans confidential when needed.
DispatchPrompt action—carrying out the decision without unnecessary delay. The original transcription spells it despatch.

Hamilton accepts debate and competing views in a legislature. He argues that requiring several executives to agree can instead produce conflicting directions and delay while a law or plan is being carried out.

Accountability: tracing responsibility

Shared executive authority can produce disagreement, conflicting directions, and blame shifting. Each official may claim someone else caused the failure, making it harder for the public to identify responsibility.

Several executives

Competing directions → a policy fails → officials blame one another.

One executive

One identifiable leader → a policy succeeds or fails → the public has a clearer person to question.

Hamilton argues that a single executive makes responsibility easier to trace. It does not mean the president personally causes every outcome or that the public always knows exactly what happened.

Two safeguards for republican government

Dependence on the people
The executive remains answerable to the people through an elected, limited-term office rather than hereditary rule. Public judgment matters to who holds office.
Responsibility for official conduct
The executive’s actions can be identified, criticized, investigated, and, when warranted, punished. Hamilton distinguishes public condemnation from legal punishment.

CONSTITUTIONAL CONTEXT

Who can hold a president accountable?

Voters influence who holds office through elections. The House can impeach—formally accuse an official. The Senate conducts the trial and can remove following conviction; conviction requires two-thirds of senators present. Voters do not conduct impeachment.

Congress’s control of funding, Senate confirmation of specified appointments, and judicial review—courts reviewing challenged government actions under the Constitution—are additional constitutional checks. They support this lesson but are not Hamilton’s list of the two safeguards in No. 70.

Lesson EQ connection

How can a president lead decisively without opening the door to tyranny?

Federalist No. 70 argues that a single, energetic executive supports decisive leadership while making responsibility easier to identify. Energy needs unity, duration, adequate support, and competent powers. Dependence on the people and responsibility for official conduct are safeguards against unchecked power.

AP exam focus

Identify Hamilton’s defense of a single, energetic executive, explain how unity can improve action, and connect identifiable responsibility to accountability. Modern unitary executive theory concerns how much control a president has over executive officials. That is a different legal debate from Hamilton’s defense of a single executive here; No. 70 does not by itself settle the scope or limits of that control.

Sources and historical context

The original essay compares executive councils and multiple executives with a single executive. Hamilton also discusses Britain’s king and council: advisers may help locate responsibility where a monarch is legally protected, but may obscure responsibility around an already accountable republican executive. This is Hamilton’s historical argument, not a claim that all monarchies work alike or that No. 70 proves every strong president protects liberty.
