Federalist No. 78

Alexander Hamilton · Publius · May 28, 1788
Historical Context
Alexander Hamilton wrote Federalist No. 78 in 1788 to defend the proposed federal judiciary. Critics feared unelected judges with secure tenure. Hamilton argued that courts needed independence to apply the Constitution even when elected officials preferred a conflicting law.

Why Hamilton called the judiciary less dangerous

"Whoever attentively considers the different departments of power must perceive, that, in a government in which they are separated from each other, the judiciary, from the nature of its functions, will always be the least dangerous to the political rights of the Constitution." The executive has the sword; the legislature has the purse. The judiciary has neither — only judgment.
Hamilton argues that courts are less dangerous because they do not command the military or control government spending. Their role is to decide legal cases. This does not mean courts lack binding authority.
"The judiciary... has no influence over either the sword or the purse; no direction either of the strength or of the wealth of the society; and can take no active resolution whatever. It may truly be said to have neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment."
Hamilton contrasts executive enforcement, legislative lawmaking and spending, and judicial judgment. He uses that division to defend judicial independence—not to claim that judicial decisions have no practical effect.

Why courts should follow the Constitution

"No legislative act... contrary to the Constitution, can be valid... The interpretation of the laws is the proper and peculiar province of the courts." Hamilton anticipates judicial review — the power of courts to strike down laws that violate the Constitution — fifteen years before Marbury v. Madison formally established it.
Judicial review means deciding whether government action violates the Constitution. Hamilton argues that courts should follow the Constitution when an ordinary statute conflicts with it.
Where the will of the legislature conflicts with the will of the people expressed in the Constitution, "the judges ought to be governed by the latter rather than the former." Hamilton argues that courts should uphold the Constitution even when a temporary legislative majority favors a conflicting law.
Hamilton argues that constitutional limits should protect people even when an elected majority favors an unconstitutional law.

Why Hamilton defended secure judicial tenure

"The standard of good behavior for the continuance in office of the judicial magistracy is certainly one of the most valuable of the modern improvements in the practice of government." Hamilton defends Article III's guarantee that federal judges hold office "during good behavior" — secure tenure unless the judge leaves office or is removed after House impeachment and Senate conviction.
Federal judges serve during good behavior. Removal requires House impeachment and Senate conviction. Hamilton argues that protection from losing office reduces pressure to please political leaders.
"That inflexible and uniform adherence to the rights of the Constitution, and of individuals, which we perceive to be indispensable in the courts of justice, can certainly not be expected from judges who hold their offices by a temporary commission." Hamilton argues that secure tenure reduces pressure to please the people who appoint judges. It supports independence without guaranteeing impartial decisions.
Hamilton defends secure tenure as a protection for independent judgment. It does not guarantee that judges will always be impartial or decide correctly.

Judicial independence means judges can decide cases without having to please elected officials to keep their positions. Article III also protects their pay from being reduced while they serve. Secure tenure and salary protection support that independence.
