The Wealth of Nations

Adam Smith · The Wealth of Nations · 1776

Selected quotations and course explanations of Smith’s arguments.

Historical Context
Adam Smith published The Wealth of Nations in 1776. He examined exchange, competition, specialization, and the role of government in economic life. These selections connect his arguments to AP Government discussions of free enterprise. Modern course connections are explanations, not Smith’s own words.

Self-interest and exchange

“It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.” Smith argues that exchange often happens because both sides expect a benefit. A baker earns income by selling bread that customers want.
Smith describes a person being “led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention.” The invisible hand is his metaphor for an unintended public benefit from individual choices. It does not mean every self-interested action helps society.

Competition and specialization

Competition means sellers must try to attract customers who have alternatives. Smith argues that competition can encourage lower prices, better products, and more efficient use of resources. A monopoly—a seller protected from competition—can instead use its position to benefit itself at others’ expense.
Division of labor means breaking production into specialized tasks. Smith’s pin-factory example describes ten workers making about 48,000 pins a day by dividing the work. Working separately without the same specialization, each would produce far fewer. Larger markets can support more specialization and exchange.

Government’s responsibilities

Smith criticizes mercantilism: policies that favor particular producers through trade restrictions and exclusive privileges. A tariff is a tax on imports; a monopoly privilege protects a seller from competitors. His criticism asks whether a policy serves the public or mainly the producers it protects.
Smith assigns government responsibilities for national defense, administering justice, and providing public works or institutions that private businesses would not adequately supply. Courts and enforcement of contracts help people trade. His defense of markets includes government responsibilities; it does not call for government to disappear.
For AP Government, connect this reading to free enterprise and equality of opportunity. Smith’s criticism of barriers to entering trades helps explain the argument for letting people compete.

Connections to American political values

Individualism
People can shape their lives through their choices. Smith’s discussion of self-interest connects to this value.
Equality of opportunity
People should have an equal chance to compete. Removing legal barriers to entering a trade illustrates that goal.
Free enterprise
Self-interest and competition guide production and the allocation of resources, with limited government regulation. Allocation means deciding how resources are used.
Rule of law
Everyone, including those in power, must follow and be accountable to the same laws. Courts and enforcement of contracts support exchange.
