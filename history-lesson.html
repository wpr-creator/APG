THE STARTING IDEA · TOPIC 1.3

THE DECLARATION SETS THE STANDARD

Americans broke from Britain because they believed concentrated power had violated their rights. The Declaration of Independence argued that people possess natural rights and that government receives authority from the consent of the governed.

WHY IT MATTERS

The Revolution created a lasting dilemma: Americans needed government, but they distrusted rulers with too much power.

THE FIRST PLAN · TOPIC 1.4

THE ARTICLES PROTECT STATE SOVEREIGNTY

The Articles of Confederation created a loose union in which the states remained sovereign and the national government stayed weak on purpose.

THE STRUCTURE

  • One-house, or unicameral, Congress
  • One vote per state
  • No independent executive
  • No national judiciary

THE LIMITS

  • Congress requested money from states
  • Congress could not tax individuals directly
  • Congress depended on states to follow its decisions
  • Amendments required all 13 states
POWER BALANCE

The system favored state sovereignty over national sovereignty.

THE BREAKDOWN · TOPIC 1.4

A GOVERNMENT TOO WEAK TO ACT

REVENUECongress could not levy taxes directly, so it struggled to pay debts and provide national defense.

COMMERCECongress could not regulate interstate commerce, so states created conflicting trade rules.

ENFORCEMENTNational laws often operated like recommendations because Congress acted through the states, not directly on individuals.

COURTS & CURRENCYNo national judiciary settled disputes, and multiple currencies made trade unstable.

Shays’ Rebellion became the clearest warning. The uprising showed that the national government lacked dependable military and financial power to protect property and maintain order.

THE REPLACEMENT · TOPIC 1.3

THE CONSTITUTION CREATES POWER—AND DIVIDES IT

Delegates met to revise the Articles but wrote a new Constitution. It created a stronger national government that could act directly on people while still limiting power through its structure.

THE BARGAINS · TOPIC 1.3

COMPROMISE MAKES AGREEMENT POSSIBLE

GREAT COMPROMISE

A bicameral Congress: representation by population in the House and equal representation for every state in the Senate.

THREE-FIFTHS COMPROMISE

Three-fifths of the enslaved population counted for representation and direct taxation. This increased slaveholding states’ power while enslaved people remained without political rights.

ELECTORAL COLLEGE

States appoint electors who formally choose the president, combining popular participation with state-based and indirect selection.

ARTICLE V

Amendments became possible without unanimity but still require supermajorities, balancing stability with political change.

AP CONNECTIONThe Great Compromise created equal state representation in the Senate and population-based representation in the House.

FEDERALIST NO. 10

James Madison argued that factions are inevitable in a free society. A large federal republic with many competing interests makes it harder for one faction to dominate.

Federalists saw stronger national power as a solution. Anti-Federalists saw that same power as a possible source of tyranny.

071788–91

THE SETTLEMENT · TOPIC 1.5

RATIFICATION AND THE BILL OF RIGHTS

1788The ninth state ratified the Constitution, allowing the new system to begin.

1789The new Congress and president began work under the Constitution.

1791The states ratified the first ten amendments: the Bill of Rights.

The promise of a Bill of Rights helped answer Anti-Federalist fears without undoing the Constitution’s expansion of national power. The settlement combined a stronger national government with explicit protections for individual liberty.

THE WHOLE STORYFear of tyranny produced a weak national government. Its failures produced a stronger Constitution. Fear of that new power produced a ratification debate and the Bill of Rights.
