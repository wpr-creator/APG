APG LESSON 2.3 / AP TOPICS 2.4–2.7

The Presidential Toolbox

Back to Unit 2 →Federalist No. 70 →

Policy agenda: The goals a president wants the government to accomplish.

Formal powers: Powers the Constitution gives the president, such as rejecting bills and commanding the armed forces.

Informal powers: Ways presidents influence government through political practice, such as negotiating with lawmakers or asking the public to support a proposal.

Presidential tools
ToolWhat the president can doMain check or limit
Regular vetoThe president can reject a bill Congress passed and send it back with reasons. This is called a veto. The bill does not become law unless Congress votes to pass it despite the president’s rejection.Congress can override the veto, making the bill law without the president’s approval. This requires a two-thirds vote in both the House and Senate.
Pocket vetoThe president leaves a bill unsigned for ten days, excluding Sundays. If Congress has stopped meeting in a way that makes it impossible to send the bill back, the bill fails. This is a pocket veto.

Adjournment means ending a meeting or session. A break allows a pocket veto only when it prevents the bill’s return.

Congress cannot override a pocket veto. Lawmakers must pass the bill again. If the president can still return the bill but simply takes no action, it becomes law after the ten-day period.
TreatyThe president can negotiate a formal agreement with another country, called a treaty. Two-thirds of senators present must agree before the president may formally approve it for the United States. That final approval is called ratification.The Senate can refuse its agreement or require changes and conditions. The Senate’s agreement is called consent. The president then decides whether to ratify the treaty.
Executive agreementThe president can make an agreement with another country without using the Senate’s two-thirds treaty vote. This is called an executive agreement. The president must have permission from the Constitution, an existing law, or an earlier treaty.The agreement must stay within that permission. Some agreements also require Congress’s approval or money Congress has agreed to provide.
Executive orderThe president can give executive officials written instructions about how to carry out government work. These instructions are called an executive order. They must use powers the Constitution or an existing federal law already gives the president.A court can stop an order that breaks the law or goes beyond the president’s powers. A later president can often change or cancel an order, while following applicable legal requirements.
Signing statementWhen signing a bill into law, the president can also issue a written explanation of what the law means to the administration or how officials plan to carry it out. This explanation is called a signing statement.The statement does not change the law or erase any of its requirements. The president has signed the bill, so it becomes law even if the statement expresses objections.
Bargaining and persuasionThe president can negotiate with lawmakers and try to convince them to support a proposal. The president might accept changes to the proposal to gain enough votes. These efforts are called bargaining and persuasion.Members of Congress decide how to vote. The president cannot order them to support a proposal, even when they belong to the same party.
Bully pulpitPresidents receive substantial public attention. They can use speeches, interviews, and social media to draw attention to problems and build support for proposals. This opportunity to influence public discussion is called the bully pulpit.

When presidents influence which problems people and government officials consider important, they are helping set the policy agenda. This is agenda setting.

People may support or reject the message. Public support can put pressure on Congress, but lawmakers still decide whether to pass a bill.
Examples
Regular veto

Hypothetical: The president returns a spending bill; lawmakers try to gather enough votes to override.

A veto threat can also persuade lawmakers to change a bill before the president receives it.

Pocket veto

Hypothetical: Congress adjourns in a way that prevents return, and the president leaves the bill unsigned through the deadline.

Treaty

Hypothetical: The president negotiates an arms-control treaty and seeks Senate consent before ratifying it.

Executive agreement

Hypothetical: A law authorizes cooperation with another country, and the president makes an agreement within that law’s limits.

Executive order

Hypothetical: A president directs agencies to coordinate disaster planning under an existing law.

Signing statement

Hypothetical: The president signs a transportation law and explains a reporting requirement without removing it.

Bargaining and persuasion

Hypothetical: The president agrees to a smaller funding request to win enough votes for a bill.

Bully pulpit

Hypothetical: A president gives a national speech supporting an education bill, hoping viewers will contact lawmakers.

Other shared powers

Appointments

The president chooses a person for an office. This proposed choice is called a nomination.

For certain jobs, the Senate must vote to approve the person. That approval is called confirmation. These jobs include Cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, and federal judges.

Many close White House advisers do not require confirmation, including the chief of staff.

Some Executive Office of the President positions require confirmation. One is the director of the Office of Management and Budget, which helps the president prepare the budget and oversee its administration.

Judicial life tenure means federal judges can serve during good behavior, allowing their influence to continue beyond a president’s term.

Commander in chief

President

Directs the armed forces.

Congress

Decides whether to declare war, passes laws approving military action, and decides what money the military receives.

Congress passed the War Powers Resolution to limit the president’s ability to keep troops fighting overseas without congressional approval. It requires communication with Congress and sets deadlines for ending certain military actions unless Congress approves their continuation.

War Powers deadlines
  • 48 hours: report covered troop deployments to Congress.
  • 60 days: generally end covered action after the report is submitted or required, unless Congress authorizes continued action or a statutory exception applies.
  • Up to 30 more days: may be available for necessary safe withdrawal.

Compare the tools
Key differences
CompareThe distinction
Regular veto / pocket vetoA regular veto returns the bill and allows an override. A pocket veto requires inaction plus adjournment preventing return; no override is possible.
Treaty / executive agreementA treaty needs Senate consent before presidential ratification. An executive agreement skips that treaty-consent vote but still needs legal authority.
Executive order / signing statementAn executive order directs executive work. A signing statement explains a signed law without changing its text.
Private bargaining / public persuasionPrivate bargaining seeks officials’ cooperation directly. Public persuasion builds attention that may pressure officials; neither commands their votes.

Other limits to know

  • No general federal line-item veto: the president cannot sign a bill while canceling selected provisions. The Supreme Court struck down the federal line-item veto in 1998.
  • Constitutional amendments do not need presidential approval: Article V gives the president no formal approval or veto role in the amendment process.
  • Conflict does not create authority: Congress’s refusal to support a policy does not itself give the president power to carry it out another way.
Sources and reference

Additional procedural details
  • A regular veto returns the bill within ten days, excluding Sundays.
  • Not every congressional recess or adjournment prevents a bill’s return.
  • Treaties may need implementation laws or funding after ratification.
  • Federal judges are independent; presidents cannot direct their decisions.
