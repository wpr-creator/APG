Presidential powers

A policy agenda is the set of goals a president seeks to advance. Each presidential tool has its own procedures and limits.

Formal powers have their basic authority in the Constitution. Informal powers include tools developed through political practice, such as bargaining and public persuasion.

Executive directives need authority from the Constitution or a law Congress passed. Authority supplied by a law is called statutory authority.

FORMAL POWER

Regular veto

Returns a bill to Congress with objections.

How it works
The president returns the bill to the chamber where it began. Before passage, a veto threat may persuade lawmakers to change a bill.
Check or limit
Congress can override the veto—pass the bill despite the president’s objection—with two-thirds in each chamber.
Example
Hypothetical: The president returns a spending bill; lawmakers try to gather enough votes to override.

In the glossary: Veto

FORMAL POWER

Pocket veto

Lets a bill fail without a signature when Congress’s adjournment prevents its return.

How it works
The president leaves the bill unsigned for ten days, excluding Sundays, and Congress’s adjournment prevents return. Both conditions must apply.
Check or limit
Congress cannot override because the bill was not returned; it must pass the bill again. If return remains possible, an unsigned bill becomes law after the ten-day period.
Example
Hypothetical: Congress adjourns in a way that prevents return, and the president leaves the bill unsigned through the deadline.

In the glossary: Pocket Veto

FORMAL POWER · SHARED WITH THE SENATE

Treaty

Makes a formal international agreement through the Constitution’s treaty process.

How it works
The president negotiates, two-thirds of senators present consent, and the president may then ratify. Ratify means formally approve the agreement for the United States.
Check or limit
The Senate can refuse consent or attach conditions. Senate consent permits ratification; it does not itself ratify the treaty.
Example
Hypothetical: The president negotiates an arms-control treaty and seeks Senate consent before ratifying it.

In the glossary: Treaty

INFORMAL FOREIGN-POLICY POWER

Executive agreement

Makes an international agreement without the Senate’s treaty-consent vote.

How it works
Legal authority may come from a law Congress passed, an existing treaty, or the president’s constitutional powers.
Check or limit
It must stay within that authority. Some agreements also need congressional approval or funding.
Example
Hypothetical: A law authorizes cooperation with another country, and the president makes an agreement within that law’s limits.

In the glossary: Executive Agreement

EXECUTIVE DIRECTIVE · EXISTING AUTHORITY REQUIRED

Executive order

Gives instructions to executive officials using authority the president already has.

How it works
The president directs government work under constitutional or statutory authority.
Check or limit
An order cannot create a power the president lacks. Courts can block unlawful action, and later presidents may revise or revoke orders within legal limits.
Example
Hypothetical: A president directs agencies to coordinate disaster planning under an existing law.

In the glossary: Executive Order

INFORMAL POWER · ACCOMPANIES SIGNING

Signing statement

Explains the president’s interpretation of a bill the president signs into law.

How it works
The president signs the bill and issues an explanation of how the administration understands or plans to carry out the law.
Check or limit
The enacted text remains in place. A signing statement is not a veto and does not delete provisions.
Example
Hypothetical: The president signs a transportation law and explains a reporting requirement without removing it.

In the glossary: Signing Statement

INFORMAL POWER · SEEKING COOPERATION

Bargaining and persuasion

Wins support by negotiating changes with lawmakers.

How it works
The president may accept changes to a proposal to win lawmakers’ support.
Check or limit
Lawmakers choose their own votes. The president cannot order Congress to support a proposal.
Example
Hypothetical: The president agrees to a smaller funding request to win enough votes for a bill.

In the glossary: Bargaining and Persuasion

INFORMAL POWER · PUBLIC COMMUNICATION

Bully pulpit

Uses the president’s public platform to draw attention to an issue.

How it works
Speeches and other public appeals can encourage people to pressure Congress. This is agenda setting: influencing which issues receive attention.
Check or limit
Public attention does not guarantee passage. Congress still decides how to vote.
Example
Hypothetical: A president gives a national speech supporting an education bill, hoping viewers will contact lawmakers.

Appointments

  • President nominates—chooses a candidate for an office.
  • Senate confirms specified appointments, including Cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, and federal judges.
  • Many close White House advisers do not require confirmation, including the chief of staff.

Some Executive Office of the President positions require confirmation. One is the director of the Office of Management and Budget, which helps the president prepare the budget and oversee its administration.

Judicial life tenure means federal judges can serve during good behavior, allowing their influence to continue beyond a president’s term.

Commander in chief

President

Directs military operations.

Congress

Declares war, authorizes military action, and controls funding.

The War Powers Resolution attempted to limit presidential troop commitments by requiring consultation with Congress, reporting, and a framework for withdrawal.

Timing reference

  • 48 hours: report covered troop deployments to Congress.
  • 60 days: generally end covered action after the report is submitted or required, unless Congress authorizes continued action or a statutory exception applies.
  • Up to 30 more days: may be available for necessary safe withdrawal.

Compare the tools

Key differences
CompareThe distinction
Regular veto / pocket vetoA regular veto returns the bill and allows an override. A pocket veto requires inaction plus adjournment preventing return; no override is possible.
Treaty / executive agreementA treaty needs Senate consent before presidential ratification. An executive agreement skips that treaty-consent vote but still needs legal authority.
Executive order / signing statementAn executive order directs executive work. A signing statement explains a signed law without changing its text.
Private bargaining / public persuasionPrivate bargaining seeks officials’ cooperation directly. Public persuasion builds attention that may pressure officials; neither commands their votes.

Other limits to know

  • No general federal line-item veto: the president cannot sign a bill while canceling selected provisions. The Supreme Court struck down the federal line-item veto in 1998.
  • Constitutional amendments do not need presidential approval: Article V gives the president no formal approval or veto role in the amendment process.
  • Conflict does not create authority: Congress’s refusal to support a policy does not itself give the president power to carry it out another way.

Additional procedural details
  • A regular veto returns the bill within ten days, excluding Sundays.
  • Not every congressional recess or adjournment prevents a bill’s return.
  • Treaties may need implementation laws or funding after ratification.
  • Federal judges are independent; presidents cannot direct their decisions.
