An agenda needs a tool

A policy agenda is the set of goals a president seeks to advance. Formal powers have their basic authority in the Constitution. Informal powers include tools developed through political practice, such as negotiation and public persuasion.

Executive directives require constitutional or statutory authority. Statutory authority means authority supplied by a law Congress passed. A tool’s name does not tell you whether a particular use is lawful: ask where the authority comes from, how the process works, and who can limit it.

Our course calls this Lesson 2.3. Its College Board content is AP Topics 2.4–2.7: presidential powers, checks, expansion, and communication. The lesson number and AP topic numbers are different labels.

01
FORMAL POWER

Regular veto

What it does
Returns a bill to Congress with objections instead of signing it into law.
How it works
The president returns the bill to the chamber where it began within ten days, excluding Sundays. A veto threat can also influence negotiations before a bill reaches the president.
Check or limit
Congress can override a regular veto—pass the bill despite the president’s objection—with a two-thirds vote in each chamber. A threat does not itself change or veto a bill.
Brief example
Hypothetical: The president threatens to veto a spending bill unless lawmakers change a provision. If the president returns the passed bill with objections, Congress can revise it or attempt an override.

In the glossary: Veto

02
FORMAL POWER

Pocket veto

What it does
Prevents a bill from becoming law through presidential inaction when Congress’s adjournment prevents its return.
How it works
The president leaves the bill unsigned for ten days, excluding Sundays, and Congress’s adjournment prevents return. Both conditions matter. If return remains possible and the president neither signs nor returns the bill within that period, it becomes law without a signature.
Check or limit
Congress cannot override a pocket veto because no bill is returned for reconsideration. It must pass the bill again to try to make it law. Not every recess or adjournment prevents return.
Brief example
Hypothetical: Congress ends its session in a way that prevents return of a bill. The president leaves it unsigned through the ten-day period, excluding Sundays. The bill fails.

In the glossary: Pocket Veto

03
FORMAL POWER · SHARED WITH THE SENATE

Treaty

What it does
Creates a formal international agreement through the Constitution’s treaty process.
How it works
The president or presidential representatives negotiate. Two-thirds of senators present must consent. After Senate consent, the president decides whether to ratify—formally approve the treaty for the United States—and completes the appropriate international steps.
Check or limit
The Senate can withhold consent or attach conditions. Senate consent permits ratification; the Senate does not itself ratify the treaty. Implementation may also require legislation or funding.
Brief example
Hypothetical: The president negotiates an arms-control treaty. Senators consider it, two-thirds present consent, and the president then proceeds with ratification.

In the glossary: Treaty

04
INTERNATIONAL AGREEMENT · DIFFERENT LEGAL BASES

Executive agreement

What it does
Makes an international agreement through a process other than the Senate’s treaty-consent procedure.
How it works
Authority may come from a statute, an existing treaty, or the president’s constitutional powers. A statute is a law Congress passed. Some agreements therefore rely on prior or later congressional approval rather than unilateral presidential authority.
Check or limit
The agreement must stay within its legal authority. It does not use the treaty-consent vote, but that does not make it free of congressional limits, funding requirements, or judicial questions.
Brief example
Hypothetical: A law authorizes cooperation with another country on a shared program. The administration negotiates an agreement within that law’s limits.

In the glossary: Executive Agreement

05
EXECUTIVE DIRECTIVE · EXISTING AUTHORITY REQUIRED

Executive order

What it does
Directs executive officials on how to carry out government work.
How it works
The president issues a directive under existing constitutional or statutory authority. Statutory authority means authority supplied by a law Congress passed. An order does not need a new congressional vote each time if existing law already supports it.
Check or limit
Courts can hear legal challenges to an order and block unlawful action. A later president may revise or revoke an order, subject to applicable legal requirements. Conflict with Congress does not create a new power.
Brief example
Hypothetical: Within authority supplied by law, a president directs agencies to coordinate disaster planning. An order could not lawfully invent a spending power Congress never provided.

In the glossary: Executive Order

06
INFORMAL TOOL · ACCOMPANIES SIGNING

Signing statement

What it does
Explains the president’s view of legislation being signed, often describing interpretation or intended implementation.
How it works
The president signs the bill into law and issues a written explanation. Unlike a veto, a signing statement accompanies approval rather than returning the bill to Congress.
Check or limit
It does not change the enacted text or let the president delete a provision. Courts interpret the law, and Congress retains its legislative and oversight powers.
Brief example
Hypothetical: The president signs a transportation law and explains how the administration reads a reporting requirement. The explanation does not erase that requirement.

In the glossary: Signing Statement

07
INFORMAL POWER · SEEKING COOPERATION

Bargaining and persuasion

What it does
Builds support through negotiations, concessions, and efforts to persuade other officials.
How it works
The president may meet privately with lawmakers, agree to changes in a proposal, or trade political support to build a group of supporters. The goal is cooperation on a policy agenda—the set of goals the president seeks to advance.
Check or limit
Presidents cannot command congressional votes. Lawmakers answer to their own districts or states, parties, and judgments; a concession does not guarantee agreement.
Brief example
Hypothetical: The president accepts a smaller funding request after meeting with lawmakers who would otherwise oppose the bill.

In the glossary: Bargaining and Persuasion

08
INFORMAL POWER · PUBLIC COMMUNICATION

Bully pulpit

What it does
Uses the president’s prominent public platform to draw attention to an issue and build support.
How it works
Speeches, the State of the Union address, interviews, and social media can influence agenda setting: which problems officials and the public treat as priorities. Public appeals may encourage constituents to pressure lawmakers.
Check or limit
Public support does not guarantee passage. A speech cannot replace legal authority, funding, or the votes Congress must cast. Audiences can reject or challenge the message.
Brief example
Hypothetical: A president gives a national speech urging an education bill, hoping viewers will contact their representatives. Congress still decides whether to pass it.

In the glossary: Bully PulpitAgenda Setting

Shared power: appointments

The president nominates; the Senate confirms specified positions. These include Cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, and federal judges. A nomination is a proposed choice, not a completed appointment. The Senate may approve, reject, or delay it.

The White House chief of staff does not require Senate confirmation. Most close White House staff are chosen directly. Some positions in the Executive Office of the President (EOP) do require confirmation—for example, the director of the Office of Management and Budget.

Federal judges’ life tenure during good behavior can extend a president’s influence beyond a presidential term. Judges are not presidential employees who must follow later instructions about how to decide a case.

Shared power: commander in chief

The president leads the armed forces; Congress retains declaration-of-war, authorization, and funding powers. Military leadership is not the same as holding every power over war.

President

Directs military operations as commander in chief.

Congress

Declares war, legislates authorizations, and provides or limits funding.

The War Powers Resolution of 1973 attempted to constrain presidential troop commitments by requiring consultation and reporting and setting a withdrawal framework. For covered deployments, reporting is required within 48 hours; the law generally requires termination within 60 days after the report is submitted or required, unless Congress authorizes continued action or another statutory exception applies. Up to 30 more days may be available for necessary safe withdrawal. Its interpretation and effectiveness remain contested.

Keep these tools straight

Similar goals, different processes
CompareThe distinction
Regular veto / pocket vetoA regular veto returns a bill with objections and allows an override. A pocket veto requires inaction plus adjournment preventing return and cannot be overridden.
Treaty / executive agreementA treaty requires consent of two-thirds of senators present before presidential ratification. An executive agreement uses another process and still needs a legal basis.
Executive order / signing statementAn order directs executive work under existing authority. A signing statement explains the president’s view when signing legislation; it does not change the enacted text.
Private bargaining / public persuasionBargaining seeks cooperation directly from officials. Public persuasion seeks attention and support from a wider audience that may pressure officials. Neither commands their votes.

Three limits that apply across the lesson

  • No general federal line-item veto: the president cannot sign a bill while canceling selected provisions. The Supreme Court struck down the federal line-item veto in 1998.
  • Constitutional amendments do not need presidential approval: Article V gives the president no formal approval or veto role in the amendment process.
  • Conflict does not create authority: Congress’s refusal to support a policy does not itself give the president power to carry it out another way.
