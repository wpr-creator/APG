THE OVAL OFFICE

The PRESIDENT leads the EXECUTIVE BRANCH, carries out federal laws, serves as commander in chief, and represents the United States at home and abroad.

 

Americans elect a president every four years through the ELECTORAL COLLEGE. A president can be elected to two terms, for a maximum of eight years under normal circumstances.

 

Since George Washington took office in 1789, 45 different people have served across 47 presidencies.

 

The job has remained. The people and the country around it have changed dramatically.

##

MANY HATS

Being PRESIDENT is more than one job. The same person takes on several roles depending on what the country needs.

 

CHIEF EXECUTIVE carries out federal laws and leads the executive branch.

 

COMMANDER IN CHIEF leads the nation's armed forces.

 

CHIEF DIPLOMAT represents the United States and works with foreign leaders.

 

HEAD OF STATE represents the country at ceremonies, celebrations, and moments of national importance.

THE EARLY POTRAITS

For much of early U.S. history, the people who became PRESIDENT had a lot in common. The first 16 presidents were all white men, and nearly all came from Protestant backgrounds.

 

Eight of the first 16 enslaved people at some point in their lives. Four of the first five presidents came from Virginia, one of the country's largest slaveholding states.

 

Political power was concentrated among a relatively narrow group of Americans.

 

Who could become president reflected who held power in the country.

BREAKING THE MOLD

For a long time, the presidential portrait changed slowly. Then some presidents began breaking patterns that had lasted for generations.

 

JOHN F. KENNEDY became the first Catholic president in 1961. BARACK OBAMA became the first Black president in 2009. In 2021, KAMALA HARRIS became the first woman to serve as vice president.

 

Other patterns remain. Every U.S. president so far has been a man, and nearly all have identified as Christian.

 

The people in power can change even when the office stays the same.

##

FRAME THE PRESIDENT

Painting a PRESIDENTIAL PORTRAIT is a big job. Artists may spend months or even years studying photographs, making sketches, planning the setting, and working through multiple versions before the final portrait is revealed.

 

The president and artist make choices about everything from clothing and expression to the background and objects in the room. The goal is not just to capture a face. It is to decide how a president will be seen and remembered.

 

And with 45 different presidents and generations of artists, some have made some very interesting choices.

##

PORTRAIT NOTES

The PRESIDENCY is a lasting part of American government, but the people who hold the office reflect a country that has changed over time.

  • PRESIDENT → leads the EXECUTIVE BRANCH through several national roles
  • Early presidents → power concentrated among a narrow group of Americans
  • Over time → the presidential portrait has slowly become more representative of a changing country

BEHIND THE FRAME

WHAT PRESIDENTIAL PORTRAITS REVEAL

GEORGE WASHINGTON

  • The famous Lansdowne Portrait is packed with symbols of the new American government.
  • Books, documents, and his sword connect Washington to both government and military leadership.
  • Gilbert Stuart also began the Athenaeum Portrait, but never finished it. It became the face of Washington you see on the $1 bill today.

THEODORE ROOSEVELT

  • Roosevelt hated his first portrait, reportedly calling it a “meowing cat.”
  • John Singer Sargent painted the replacement after catching Roosevelt gripping a staircase post.
  • That spontaneous pose became the official White House portrait.

 

JOHN F. KENNEDY

  • Elaine de Kooning painted JFK while he was president in 1963, using several sessions with him to capture his restless energy.
  • After his assassination, Aaron Shikler created the official White House portrait with Kennedy looking downward, arms crossed, rather than confidently facing the viewer.

RONALD REAGAN

  • Reagan didn't like his first completed portrait, so another artist got the job.
  • The replacement shows him smiling and standing casually beside a column.
  • Even presidents ask for a retake.

BARACK OBAMA

  • Obama has strikingly different portraits in the White House and National Portrait Gallery collections.
  • Robert McCurdy's White House portrait is almost empty, with Obama against a plain white background.
  • Kehinde Wiley's portrait surrounds him with bright greenery and flowers tied to his personal history.
  • Same president. Two completely different ways to tell his story.
