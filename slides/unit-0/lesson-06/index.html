THE THIRD BRANCH

The JUDICIAL BRANCH begins with ARTICLE III, the shortest of the Constitution’s three articles creating the branches of government.

The Constitution creates one SUPREME COURT, but gives CONGRESS the power to create lower federal courts. Much of the court system we know today was built after the Constitution was written.

Unlike Congress or the President, federal courts do not make or carry out laws. They interpret laws and the CONSTITUTION when deciding real cases and disputes. Through JUDICIAL REVIEW, courts can also decide whether laws or government actions violate the Constitution.

GET THE ROBE

You cannot run for federal judge. There is no national election and no campaign asking Americans for their votes. That is how the Framers intended it: federal judges are insulated from electoral politics—at least in theory.

The PRESIDENT nominates federal judges and Supreme Court justices. The SENATE then votes on whether to confirm them.

Once confirmed, Article III judges receive LIFETIME TENURE. Tenure means how long someone may hold a position. The Constitution says judges serve during “good Behaviour,” meaning they may remain for life unless they leave office or are impeached and removed.

MEET THE COURTS

The CONSTITUTION created one Supreme Court and left the rest to Congress. So what did CONGRESS build? A federal court system that works like a pyramid.

94 DISTRICT COURTS sit at the bottom, where most federal cases begin, with roughly 670 federal judges.

13 COURTS OF APPEALS review decisions from lower courts, with 179 federal judges. An APPEAL asks a higher court to review whether the lower court made a legal mistake.

At the very top sits 1 SUPREME COURT with just 9 JUSTICES.

MAKE THE CASE

Thousands of cases ask the SUPREME COURT to hear them each year. Only about 70–80 typically receive oral arguments.

The Supreme Court is not another trial. There is no jury, no witnesses taking the stand, and usually no new evidence. The basic facts of the case have already been established in lower courts.

Instead, the justices focus on a specific CONSTITUTIONAL QUESTION or question of federal law. Both sides submit written BRIEFS, and lawyers answer questions from the justices during ORAL ARGUMENTS.

The justices consider the law, the CONSTITUTION, and earlier court decisions called PRECEDENTS.

THE FINAL WORD

After hearing a case, the nine SUPREME COURT justices discuss it and vote. Usually, five votes are enough to decide which side wins.

The MAJORITY OPINION becomes the Court’s official decision. It explains the ruling and the reasoning behind it. Justices who disagree can write a DISSENTING OPINION explaining why they believe the Court got it wrong.

That ruling can become PRECEDENT, guiding courts when similar cases appear later. This is how one case can shape what a law or constitutional right means for millions of people. 

THE COURT'S REASONING

Courts give laws and constitutional rights meaning by applying the CONSTITUTION to real disputes and explaining their decisions.

  • JUDICIAL REVIEW → courts can check whether government actions follow the Constitution
  • SUPREME COURT → only a small number of cases reach the nine justices
  • PRECEDENT → rulings help guide how similar cases are decided later
