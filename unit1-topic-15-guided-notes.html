Unit 1 · Topic 1.5

FEDERALISM
GUIDED NOTES

How does the Constitution divide power between the national government and the states—and what happens when those powers collide?

Open each section as your class reaches it. Add your own examples to the writing prompts; the goal is to explain the constitutional rule, not just name it.

1. THE BIG IDEA: TWO LEVELS OF GOVERNMENT

Federalism

Federalism divides governing authority between a national government and state governments. It is different from a unitary system, where the national government holds most power, and from a confederation, where states hold most power.

The Constitution gives each level jobs to do. That division can protect local choice, but it can also create disagreement over who gets to make a rule.

In your own words: Why might a large country choose federalism instead of putting every decision in one place?
2. WHICH POWERS BELONG TO WHICH LEVEL?

National powers

ENUMERATED POWERS are powers specifically listed in the Constitution, especially Article I, Section 8. Examples include regulating interstate commerce, coining money, and raising an army.

IMPLIED POWERS are not named word-for-word but are reasonably connected to an enumerated power through the Necessary and Proper Clause.

State and shared powers

RESERVED POWERS are powers kept by the states under the Tenth Amendment, such as running elections, operating schools, and issuing many professional licenses. CONCURRENT POWERS are powers both levels share, including taxing, borrowing money, establishing courts, and enforcing laws.

Sort it: Choose one enumerated, reserved, and concurrent power. Explain why each belongs in that category.
3. WHEN NATIONAL AND STATE RULES CONFLICT

SUPREMACY CLAUSE

The SUPREMACY CLAUSE in Article VI says that the Constitution, valid federal laws, and treaties are the supreme law of the land. A conflicting state law must give way—but only when the federal government is acting within its constitutional authority.

Key Court cases

In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), the Supreme Court upheld Congress’s power to create a national bank and rejected Maryland’s attempt to tax it. In United States v. Lopez (1995), the Court ruled that Congress had gone beyond its commerce power by regulating gun possession near schools.

Apply the rule: Why do these two cases point in different directions about national power?
4. HOW THE BALANCE CHANGES

Grants and mandates

Federal grants give money to states. Categorical grants require money to be used for a specific purpose; block grants give states more flexibility within a broad policy area. A mandate requires states to follow a federal rule. An unfunded mandate does not provide enough federal money to cover the cost.

These tools show that federalism is not fixed: the balance of power shifts through laws, funding, court decisions, and political choices.

Connect: How could federal funding influence a state decision even if the state runs the program?

Before you leave

Be ready to define federalism, identify an enumerated, reserved, and concurrent power, and use the Supremacy Clause to explain a conflict between a valid federal law and a state law.
