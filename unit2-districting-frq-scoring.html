QUESTION 1

Baker & Wesberry

0 / 4 points checked

Unequal populations and the courts’ power to hear a challenge.

Check the points your response earns

Identify the difference between the districts: Baker concerned Tennessee’s state legislature; Wesberry concerned Georgia’s U.S. House districts. Connect the difference to the kind of representation affected.

Student examples and why they earn credit

EXAMPLE 1

Baker was about seats in Tennessee’s state government. Wesberry was about seats in the U.S. House, so its ruling affected representation in Congress.

Why it earns credit: Names both kinds of district and explains why the difference matters.

EXAMPLE 2

In Baker, voters challenged how their state lawmakers were elected. In Wesberry, they challenged how their members of Congress were elected. The cases affected different levels of government.

Why it earns credit: Uses everyday language to identify the same meaningful difference.

Not enough yet: “They happened in different states” names a difference, but does not explain the difference in representation.

Describe relevant information about Baker: an accurate fact or its holding can earn the first B point. You do not need both for this point.

Student examples and why they earn credit

EXAMPLE 1

Tennessee had not updated its state legislative districts even though the population had shifted, leaving some voters with less representation.

Why it earns credit: Gives a relevant fact about the unequal representation Baker challenged.

EXAMPLE 2

Baker said federal courts could hear a challenge to unequal state districts under equal protection.

Why it earns credit: Accurately describes the decision.

Not enough yet: “Baker was about voting” is too vague. Identify the unequal districts or what the Court allowed.

Explain the similarity in court review: Baker let federal courts hear an unequal-representation claim, and Wesberry also involved court review of unequal district populations. A relevant Baker description alone earns 1 B point; an accurate comparison earns 2 B points total.

Student examples and why they earn credit

EXAMPLE 1

Baker let voters take unequal state districts to federal court. In Wesberry, the Court also heard a challenge about unequal districts, but those were U.S. House districts. Both allowed judges to address unequal representation.

Why it earns credit: Connects the decisions through court review without claiming Baker itself required equal populations.

EXAMPLE 2

Both involved voters who said unequal populations made their votes count less. Baker allowed that kind of state-district claim to be heard. Wesberry also allowed review and then required roughly equal populations in congressional districts.

Why it earns credit: Uses the shared problem to explain the connection and keeps the outcomes distinct.

Not enough yet: “Both had unfair districts” identifies a shared issue but does not explain the decisions.

Explain how the earlier Baker decision likely guided the Court in Wesberry. Stare decisis means following precedent—an earlier decision that guides later cases. Apply it to the Court’s ability to hear the challenge.

Student examples and why they earn credit

EXAMPLE 1

Baker had already said unequal-representation claims could be heard in federal court. Following that earlier decision helped the Court hear the challenge in Wesberry too.

Why it earns credit: Explains how a specific earlier decision guided the later case.

EXAMPLE 2

The justices could use Baker as a precedent instead of treating unequal districts as something only politicians could fix. That supported hearing Wesberry’s claim.

Why it earns credit: Applies precedent to court review; repeating the Latin term is not required.

Not enough yet: “Stare decisis means precedent” defines the term but does not apply it to these cases.

QUESTION 2

Shaw & Bush v. Vera

0 / 4 points checked

Race in district boundaries and Equal Protection.

Check the points your response earns

Identify the Equal Protection Clause, also called the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Student examples and why they earn credit

EXAMPLE 1

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Why it earns credit: Names the correct constitutional clause.

EXAMPLE 2

The Equal Protection Clause.

Why it earns credit: The clause’s name is enough for this identify question; no paragraph is needed.

Not enough yet: “The Fourteenth Amendment” alone does not identify which clause. “Equal rights” is also too vague.

Describe relevant information about Shaw: an accurate fact or its holding can earn the first B point.

Student examples and why they earn credit

EXAMPLE 1

North Carolina drew a long, narrow district connecting Black communities to create a district where Black voters were the majority.

Why it earns credit: Gives a relevant fact about District 12.

EXAMPLE 2

The Court let voters continue their claim that North Carolina had grouped them mainly by race when drawing the district.

Why it earns credit: Accurately describes what Shaw allowed; it does not claim the district was finally struck down.

Not enough yet: “Shaw banned districts with mostly Black voters” is incorrect. A majority-minority district is not automatically unconstitutional.

Use facts from both cases to explain the similar constitutional concern: race taking priority in drawing districts required a strong justification under equal protection. Strict scrutiny asks whether the state had a compelling reason and used race no more than necessary. Relevant Shaw information alone earns 1 B point; the explained comparison earns 2 B points total.

Student examples and why they earn credit

EXAMPLE 1

North Carolina connected Black communities in an unusually shaped district. Texas also used race to draw its districts instead of following ordinary boundaries. Both raised the question of whether the state had a strong enough reason for making race drive the lines. Shaw let the challenge continue; Bush upheld striking down the Texas districts.

Why it earns credit: Uses facts from both cases and explains their shared equal-protection concern while distinguishing the results.

EXAMPLE 2

In Shaw, voters said race was the main reason for District 12’s lines. In Bush, Texas used racial data and split communities to reach racial goals. In both, using race this way required a very strong reason and a map that went no further than needed.

Why it earns credit: Explains the shared constitutional test in familiar language. Naming strict scrutiny alone would not explain the connection.

Not enough yet: “Both districts looked weird” does not connect their facts to the constitutional concern. Race being considered at all does not automatically make a map invalid.

Explain a specific way the Bush decision could affect congressional redistricting in another state. Connect a mapmaking choice to avoiding racial districting that cannot be justified.

Student examples and why they earn credit

EXAMPLE 1

Another state might keep towns together instead of splitting them just to reach a racial target. That could help it avoid a challenge that race was the main reason for its lines.

Why it earns credit: Identifies an action and explains why the decision could lead to it.

EXAMPLE 2

If a state makes race the main reason for its boundaries, it would need a very strong legal reason and would have to show it used race only as much as needed. Otherwise, a court could reject its map like the Texas maps.

Why it earns credit: Explains how the ruling changes what another state must justify.

Not enough yet: “States should draw fair maps” needs a specific change and a reason. “States can never consider race” overstates the rule.
