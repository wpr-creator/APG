QUESTION 1

Baker & Wesberry

0 / 4 points checked

Unequal populations and the courts’ power to hear a challenge.

Check the points your response earns

Identify the different type of district involved. Baker challenged Tennessee’s state legislative districts. Wesberry challenged Georgia’s U.S. House districts.

Read a sample response

Baker involved representation in a state legislature, while Wesberry involved representation in the U.S. House. That difference mattered because Wesberry’s equal-population ruling applied to congressional districts.

Accurately describe Baker’s decision. Federal courts could hear an Equal Protection challenge to unequal state legislative representation.

Explain the shared decision about court review. Both cases allowed federal courts to address challenges involving unequal district populations. Describe Baker accurately to earn both B points.

Read a sample response

Baker said federal courts could hear voters’ Equal Protection challenge to unequal state districts. Wesberry also allowed court review of an unequal-population challenge, this time involving congressional districts. Wesberry went further and required U.S. House districts to have populations as equal as practicable.

Explain how Baker’s earlier decision helped shape Wesberry. Stare decisis means following precedent—an earlier court decision that guides a later one. Connect that idea to the courts’ ability to hear the case.

Read a sample response

Baker set a precedent that federal courts could hear unequal-representation claims. Wesberry followed that precedent on court review, then used Article I, Section 2 to decide that U.S. House districts must have roughly equal populations.

Keep the decisions straight. Baker opened the courthouse door. Wesberry decided the population rule for U.S. House districts. Reynolds v. Sims later established the population rule for state legislatures. Baker was decided in 1962 and Wesberry in 1964.

QUESTION 2

Shaw & Bush v. Vera

0 / 4 points checked

Race in district boundaries and Equal Protection.

Check the points your response earns

Identify the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. This clause requires states to treat people equally under the law.

Use an accurate fact and decision from Shaw. North Carolina drew an unusually shaped district connecting Black communities. The Court allowed voters’ claim that the lines improperly separated people by race to proceed.

Use facts from both cases to explain why race in mapmaking raised an Equal Protection problem. Connect race taking priority over ordinary districting rules to strict scrutiny, the Court’s demanding review of the state’s justification. Describe Shaw accurately to earn both B points.

Read a sample response

In Shaw, North Carolina’s narrow, irregular District 12 connected Black communities. In Bush v. Vera, Texas used racial data and departed from ordinary districting rules to draw the challenged districts. Both raised the concern that race drove the lines, requiring strong justification under Equal Protection. Shaw let the claim proceed. Bush upheld the judgment striking down three Texas districts.

Explain one way another state would change its mapmaking to follow the decision. Connect a specific action to avoiding unjustified racial districting.

Read a sample response

A state could place more weight on keeping towns together instead of splitting them to meet a racial target. If race still predominates, the state must show a compelling reason and boundaries closely fitted to that reason. This makes a map more likely to survive strict scrutiny.

Keep the decisions straight. Shaw did not finally strike down District 12 in 1993. Using race in any way does not automatically invalidate a map. When race predominates, the state must satisfy strict scrutiny.
